MODULE 1.3

✍️ Prompt Engineering

“Engineering” sounds intimidating, but the idea is simple: ask well. You’ll learn the parts of a good prompt, how to use examples, how to specify the response format, how to improve through multiple rounds, what to do with difficult tasks, and how to save prompts that work so you never have to write them from scratch again.

1

🎯 Why the prompt matters

Remember Module 1.1: the model chooses the most likely continuation of the text it receives. If the request is “write about marketing,” thousands of continuations are likely—for a student, for a large company, in a teaching tone, in an advertising tone. The AI chooses one of them, almost always the most generic. A weak answer isn’t the AI being lazy: the request left everything open.

🌱 New here?

Prompt is everything you write (or paste or attach) to the AI in a message. Prompt engineering is the habit of deliberately putting that request together with the information the AI needs, instead of typing the first sentence that comes to mind. Output is the answer the AI returns.

What it is

Prompt engineering means writing a request that leaves little doubt about what you want: for whom, for what purpose, in what format, and within what limits.

✗ Vague request

“Create a social media post.”

  • ✗Which platform? What topic?
  • ✗For whom, and with what goal?
  • ✗Length, tone, and call to action?

✓ Clear request

“Write an Instagram post for my bakery announcing the naturally leavened bread that arrives every Friday at 7 a.m. Audience: neighborhood residents. Tone: welcoming, without overdoing it. Up to 80 words; end by inviting people to try it.”

  • ✓Platform, topic, and audience defined.
  • ✓Tone and length agreed on.
  • ✓Ends with a concrete action.

Why learn

The same assistant seems “dumb” to someone who writes a poor prompt and “brilliant” to someone who writes a good one. Writing a complete prompt takes one extra minute and saves several rounds of “that’s not what I meant.” It’s the skill that most improves your results with AI, in any tool.

🧱 Prompt anatomy

A good request usually has six parts. You don’t always need all of them—for “translate this sentence,” the task is enough—but when the result matters, mentally go through this list: role, context, task, examples, format, and constraints.

The most important part is context. Write as if you were explaining the task to a capable coworker who just joined the company: they’re skilled, but they know nothing about your customer, your product, or what happened yesterday. The “role” (“you’re a sales consultant”) helps set the tone, but on its own it doesn’t replace context.

What it is

Prompt anatomy is a six-part checklist—role, context, task, examples, format, and constraints—that you use to put together complete requests.

Goal: use all six parts in a real request from your work.

Role: you’re <e.g., an e-commerce customer service specialist>.

Context: <who you are, what the business does, who the audience is, what happened>

Task: <verb + what to do. E.g., write 3 versions of a reply to the
message below>

Example of what I like: <paste an old reply that worked, or delete
this line>

Format: <e.g., numbered list, each version up to 60 words>

Constraints: <e.g., don’t promise a timeline, don’t use slang, address the reader as “you”>

Material: <paste the message or text here, without personal information>

How to check: Read the answer with the list of constraints beside it and mark each one as met or not met. Then delete the context line and try again in a new conversation: the difference between the two answers shows how much context matters.

Why learn

Once you know the anatomy, you can stop relying on “magic prompts” copied from the internet. You can build your own for your situation, and diagnose what’s missing when the answer falls short: it’s almost always context, format, or a constraint.

sets tone and focus

3

📎 Show with examples

Describing a style in words is hard: “light but professional tone” means different things to different people. Showing is easier. When you paste two or three examples of what you consider good, the AI picks up the pattern—length, vocabulary, structure—and repeats it. This is one of the most effective techniques there is.

🌱 New here?

No examples (in English, zero-shot) means asking without showing any examples. A few examples (few-shot) means pasting a few “input → output” pairs before your request so the AI can imitate the pattern. Reference material is a document you attach for the AI to consult: company policy, a menu, a handbook.

What it is

Using examples means including a few models of the expected result in your request, so the AI can copy the pattern instead of guessing what you meant by vague words.

Try this: classify customer messages using three examples.

Classify customer messages as: QUESTION, COMPLAINT, COMPLIMENT, or REQUEST.
Respond only with the category and a summary of up to 8 words.

Examples:
Message: "Do you deliver to Niterói?"
Response: QUESTION — asks about delivery area

Message: "My order arrived with a dented box."
Response: COMPLAINT — product arrived with damaged packaging

Message: "I loved Júlia’s customer service. She was so attentive!"
Response: COMPLIMENT — praises attendant for being attentive

Now classify:
<paste 5 to 10 real messages here, without names or phone numbers>

How to check: All responses must follow exactly the pattern in the examples (CATEGORY — short summary). Check that the category makes sense for each one. If a message is ambiguous, add a fourth example similar to it and run it again.

✓ Examples that help

  • ✓Two to five, varied from one another.
  • ✓All in the same format you want to receive.
  • ✓Taken from your real work, with identifying details removed.

✗ Examples that get in the way

  • ✗All alike: the AI copies too much and loses variety.
  • ✗With errors: the AI faithfully repeats the error.
  • ✗Third-party text you only want to "copy" — watch out for copyright.

Why learn

Examples solve in a second what ten lines of description can’t. It’s the fastest way to get the AI to write “your way,” follow your company’s standards, or classify things exactly as you would.

Key concepts

Zero-shot
request without an example
Few-shot
a few examples first
Pattern
what the AI imitates
Reference
attached document

📐 Output format

Half the work after the AI responds is reorganizing: turning paragraphs into a table, shortening them, changing the tone. You can skip that step by asking for the format right from the start. AI follows formats very well— as long as you say which one.

Think about where the response will end up. Is it going into a spreadsheet? Ask for a table with named columns. Is it for WhatsApp? Ask for short text with no formatting. Is it for a presentation? Ask for titles and bullet points of up to ten words. The destination determines the format.

What it is

Output format is the shape of the response—its structure, length, tone, and language—specified in the request itself, based on where the text will be used.

DestinationAsk for it this wayExample wording
SpreadsheetTable with fixed columns"A table with the columns Product | Price | Notes."
WhatsAppShort text, no formatting"Up to 3 sentences, no bold, no lists."
PresentationTitle + short bullet points"5 slides: a title and 3 bullet points of up to 10 words each."
Formal emailSubject + body + sign-off"Write a subject, a body of up to 120 words, and a sign-off."
Quick decisionAnswer first, details afterward"Start with yes or no and one sentence explaining why."

💡 Tip

Say what you want, not just what you don’t want. "Don’t be long-winded" works less well than "respond in up to 80 words." Specific numbers (words, items, lines) are followed much more faithfully than adjectives like "short" or "detailed."

✗ Vague format

  • ✗"Write a good summary."
  • ✗"Don’t write too much."

✓ Specific format

  • ✓"A summary in 5 one-line bullet points, from most urgent to least urgent."
  • ✓"Up to 100 words, in plain English."

Why learn

Asking for the right format turns the AI’s response into something you can use as is, without rework. It’s the detail that separates “the AI gave me some text” from “the AI gave me the table ready to paste into the spreadsheet.”

Key concepts

Destination
defines the format
Specific number
better than an adjective
Tone
formal, friendly, technical
Answer first
conclusion before details

🔁 Iterate in rounds

The first response is rarely the final one, and that’s okay. That’s what conversation is for: you read it, say what to change, and the AI adjusts. The difference between people who get value from it and those who give up comes down to how you give that feedback. "Make this better" is as vague as the vague request in topic 1; "the second paragraph sounds too formal—rewrite it as if you were talking to a neighbor" is feedback that works.

One useful technique is to reverse the direction: instead of guessing what’s missing, ask the AI to ask questions before answering. It lists what it needs to know, you answer, and the first draft comes much closer to the target.

What it is

Iterating means improving the response in short rounds: ask, read critically, give specific feedback, and ask again until you reach a version you would use.

Try this: have the AI ask what’s missing before writing.

I need <e.g., an introductory text about my company for the website>.

Before writing anything, ask me the questions you need
so you can get it right on the first try—no more than 5, numbered, one line each.
Wait for my answers.

After writing, end with:
- 2 weaknesses you see in your own version;
- 1 question about something that could still be improved.

How to check: The questions should be about things only you know (audience, differentiator, tone), not generic details. Compare the final version with one written without the questions: the version with questions usually needs fewer rounds of revisions.

Why learn

If you expect perfection in the first response, you’ll get frustrated and give up. If you treat the first response as a draft and know how to give feedback, you’ll get a quality result in a few rounds — and learn with each round what to include in your next request.

Key concepts

Round
ask, read, adjust
Specific feedback
what and where to change
Questions first
the AI asks what’s missing
Self-critique
the AI points out weaknesses

🧠 Difficult tasks

Some tasks are too big for a single request: analyzing a contract, putting together a business plan, comparing five suppliers. If you throw everything in at once, the response comes out shallow or mixes things together. Three techniques solve most cases: break it into steps, ground it in the material e use reasoning mode.

🌱 New here?

Reasoning model is a version of the assistant that “thinks” for a while before responding, trying different approaches and correcting itself. In current assistants, it appears as a “think more” option or as a separate model in the selector. It’s slower, but gets logic, calculations, and analysis right more often. Grounding means telling the AI to answer only based on the material you pasted, without filling in what it “thinks” is true.

What it is

These techniques help with complex requests: break the task into linked steps, keep the response tied to the material you provide, and turn on extended reasoning when the task requires logic.

✂️ Break it into steps

First, “list the main points,” then “analyze point 2,” then “write the recommendation.” Each step builds on the previous one. You check along the way.

📄 Ground it

“Answer only based on the document below. If the answer isn’t in it, say ‘not included.’” This greatly reduces made-up information.

🧮 Reasoning

For calculations, logic, and comparisons, turn on think-more mode. Also ask it to show the main steps so you can check where it went wrong, if it did.

Goal: analyze a long document without the AI making things up.

I’ll give you a document: <e.g., commercial lease>.
Main rule: use ONLY what’s in the document. If something isn’t included,
write “not included in the document.” Cite the passage (clause or page)
for each statement.

Step 1 — List the 8 most important points, one line each.
Step 2 — Mark which of these points involve a deadline, penalty, or money.
Step 3 — List the questions I should take to a professional
          (lawyer, accountant) before signing.

Do only Step 1 now and wait for me to ask for the next one.

Document:
<paste the text here, without the parties’ personal information>

How to check: open the document and check that each cited passage exists and says what the response claims. If any statement comes without a citation, ask: “show me the exact passage.” Remember: AI can help you read, but it doesn’t replace a professional for legal or financial decisions.

💡 Tip

Give the AI permission to say “I don’t know.” A phrase like “if you’re not sure, say so” at the end of your request reduces made-up answers that sound certain.

Why learn

Difficult tasks are exactly where you save the most time when things go well — and where things cause the most trouble when they go wrong. Breaking down, grounding, and reasoning let you use AI for serious work, with checkpoints along the way.

📚 Your prompt library

After a few weeks, you’ll notice you ask for the same things: replying to a customer, summarizing a meeting, creating a post, editing text. Writing those requests from scratch every time is a waste. The solution is a prompt library: a document with your best requests, each with marked spaces for what changes.

Assistants can also save set instructions for you: custom instructions (apply to all conversations) and projects instructions (apply within a topic). Put what you repeat in every conversation — who you are, the tone you prefer — there. Module 1.4 covers this in more depth.

What it is

A prompt library is an organized collection of requests that worked, written as templates with variables, ready to copy, fill in, and use.

Step 1 · Collect. Whenever a request works really well, copy it into the “My Prompts” document.

Step 2 · Generalize. Replace specific details with variables inside < >: <customer>, <product>, <tone>.

Step 3 · Name it and say when to use it. A clear title and one line saying when to use it.

Step 4 · Review. Every month, improve the templates you use most with what you’ve learned from each round.

Goal: turn a request that worked into a reusable template.

Below is a request that worked really well for me.
Turn it into a reusable TEMPLATE:

1. Keep the structure and what made it work.
2. Replace everything specific with variables inside < >,
   with an example value for each one.
3. Use the six parts: role, context, task, examples, format, constraints.
4. Give it a short title and a “Use when: ...” line.
5. At the end, say what kind of similar request should NOT use this template.

Original request:
<paste the successful prompt here>

How to check: Fill in the template with a NEW case, different from the original, and run it in a new conversation. If the response is just as good as the first one, add the template to your library. If not, see which variable was unclear and add an example for it.

💡 Tip

Start small: three templates for the three tasks you repeat most. A short library you use is worth more than a list of a hundred downloaded prompts you never open.

Why learn

A library turns one good response into a repeatable result. It’s also easy to share with your team: everyone starts asking in the same way, and quality no longer depends on who’s typing.
