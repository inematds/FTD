🧭 Where AI creates value

Start with a truth people rarely advertise: no one pays for “AI.” People pay to have a problem solved — less time spent, more customers served, a finished document, a mistake avoided. AI is your tool, like a spreadsheet or a car. What you sell is the result.

In practice, there are four paths. The first, often overlooked, is using AI to work more efficiently in the job you already have — money saved is money, too. The other three involve selling something new: a service delivered with AI, a “packaged” service with a fixed scope and price, or a product that sells without depending on your hours.

What it is

Making money with AI means using this course’s tools to deliver a result someone is willing to pay for—or to reduce costs in your own business. Each path trades speed of return for the ability to grow without you.

⚙️ Internal efficiency

Use AI in your own work or business to do more with the same team. The return comes from saving time and money.

🤝 AI-assisted service

Writing, customer service, data organization, automations for clients. You sell hours, with higher productivity.

📦 Productized service

The same service with a fixed scope, timeline, and price—“20 product descriptions in 5 days.”

🧩 Digital product or software

Templates, courses, automation kits, or an app. It sells without you, but requires an audience and takes longer to make the first sale.

Why learn

Knowing the paths helps you avoid the most common beginner’s mistake: jumping straight to a product (an app, a course) without ever solving a real customer’s problem. Services teach you what the market wants; the product comes later, backed by that learning.

🎯 Choose a problem

“I want to make money with AI” isn’t a plan. “Help physical therapy clinics in my neighborhood respond to appointment messages after hours” is. The difference comes down to three things: a specific audience, a concrete pain point and a realistic way for you to reach those people.

🌱 New here?

Niche is a small, clearly defined group of customers with similar needs. Pain point is a problem these people already feel, one that costs them time or money and that they already try to solve somehow. Value proposition is a sentence that says who you help, what pain point you solve, and what result you deliver.

What it is

Choosing a problem means deciding, before picking any tools, who you’ll serve and what difficulty you’ll solve for them. The best starting point is usually an area you already know: your profession, your industry, your city.

✓ Good problem

  • ✓It happens every week, not once a year.
  • ✓The person already spends time or money on it.
  • ✓You understand the industry and speak the customer’s language.
  • ✓You can talk to ten of these people this month.

✗ Warning sign

  • ✗“It works for any business.”
  • ✗You’re the only one who thinks it’s a problem.
  • ✗The customer can solve it for free in five minutes with ChatGPT.
  • ✗You don’t know anyone in the target audience.

Goal: generate and filter problem ideas based on your experience—not generic lists.

I want to find a problem I can solve for other people with AI.
My professional experience: <field, years, what I do well>
People I can contact: <e.g., shop owners in my city, accountant colleagues>
Tools I already know how to use: <e.g., ChatGPT, Claude, simple automations>
Time available per week: <hours>

1. List 8 recurring problems this audience is likely to have.
2. For each one, say how it’s solved today (without AI) and describe qualitatively how much time it takes.
3. Rate from 1 to 5: frequency, pain point, my advantage, ease of reaching the audience.
4. Remove those the customer could solve on their own with a free chatbot.
5. Write a one-sentence value proposition for the 2 best ones.
Don’t make up market numbers; when you don’t know, say “needs verification.”

How to check: The scores are AI guesses—the confirmation comes in Topic 3, by talking to real people. Check that no problem was justified with an unsourced statistic. If the value proposition doesn’t name a specific audience, ask for it to be rewritten.

Why learn

A well-chosen problem makes everything that follows easier: you know who to talk to, what to offer, and what to charge. A vague problem makes you compete with everyone and with free tools themselves. Your advantage isn’t knowing how to use AI—lots of people do—it’s knowing how to use AI in an industry you know.

Key concepts

Niche
small, clearly defined audience
Pain point
problem that already costs a lot
Value proposition
who, what pain point, what result
Advantage
the industry you know

🧪 Validate before you build

Validating means finding out, with as little effort as possible, whether someone would actually pay based on what you want to offer. The classic mistake is spending weeks building a website, logo, and automations, only to discover at launch that nobody wanted them. Validation reverses the order: evidence first, then building.

🌱 New here?

MVP (from Minimum Viable Product, the simplest version that already delivers the result—sometimes it’s you doing by hand, with AI, what will one day be automated. Pre-sale means receiving payment (or a firm commitment) before the delivery is fully ready. Pilot means serving a first customer under special terms so you can learn.

What it is

Validation means gathering signs of real demand, from the weakest (a compliment) to the strongest (payment). You invest time and money in building only when strong signals appear.

Step 1 · List. Write down the names of 15 to 20 people in your chosen audience whom you can contact.

Step 2 · Conversations. Ask for 20 minutes to understand how they deal with the problem today. Don’t sell anything yet; listen.

Step 3 · Sample. Use AI to create a small, free sample of the result, based on a real case from that person (with anonymized data).

Step 4 · Offer. Offer a paid pilot with a clear scope and price. Their response to this offer is your validation.

Goal: prepare a discovery conversation that reveals the real pain point, without questions that lead the person to a particular answer.

I’m going to spend 20 minutes talking with potential customers to understand a problem.
Audience: <e.g., owners of small real estate agencies>
Problem I suspect they have: <e.g., it takes too long to write property listings>

1. Write 8 open-ended questions about the person’s past and present
   (how they handled it last time, how long it took, what they’ve already tried, how much they’ve spent).
2. DO NOT include hypothetical questions like “would you buy...?” or questions that mention my solution.
3. Write a short, polite message asking for a conversation without making it sound like a sales pitch.
4. Create a table for me to fill out after each conversation:
   Person | How often the problem occurs | How they solve it today | Have they paid for a solution? | Memorable quote | Signal (1 to 5)

How to check: Reread the questions and cross out any that could be answered with “sure, sounds good.” After 5 conversations, look at the “Have they paid for a solution?” column: if nobody has ever spent time or money on the problem, the pain point may be smaller than it seems.

Why learn

Validation takes a few weeks of conversations; skipping validation can cost you months of work on a product with no buyers. AI has made building cheaper, which makes the temptation to build first even stronger—and validation even more important. An early “no” saves you money; it isn’t a failure.

Key concepts

Validation
evidence before effort
MVP
the minimum that delivers
Discovery conversation
listen to the past, not the “what if”
Paid pilot
the strongest signal

🏷️ Set the price

People just starting out often price too low because they’re afraid of losing the customer. With AI, the risk increases: because the work is faster, charging less can seem fair. But the customer isn’t paying for your hours—they’re paying for the result. If a listing takes 20 minutes instead of 2 hours, that’s your efficiency—not an automatic discount.

What it is

Pricing means choosing an amount based on three reference points: the floor (your costs and the minimum you need to charge for your time), the market (what similar alternatives charge), and the ceiling (how much the result is worth to the customer). A healthy price falls between the market rate and the ceiling.

How to chargeWhen to use itWatch out
HourlyUnclear scope, consulting.AI makes you faster, and you earn less.
Per projectClear deliverable: one website, 30 descriptions.Put in writing what’s outside the scope.
Fixed packageProductized, repeatable service.Limit the number of revisions.
Monthly feeOngoing work: posts, customer support, reports.Include costs for tools that may increase.

Goal: calculate the floor and organize your pricing logic using your numbers, not made-up ones.

Help me set the price for a service. Use ONLY the numbers I provide.
Service: <description and what it delivers>
Hours I spend per delivery (with AI): <hours>
Minimum hourly income I want: <R$>
Monthly tool costs: <R$> | Expected deliveries per month: <number>
Estimated taxes and fees: <percentage, confirm with your accountant>
What the client does today without me and how much it costs them: <describe>
Prices of alternatives I found: <amounts and where I saw them>

1. Calculate the FLOOR price per delivery, showing the math step by step.
2. Summarize the MARKET benchmark using the amounts I provided.
3. Describe the VALUE to the client and what I still need to ask them to estimate it.
4. Suggest 3 pricing options (basic, standard, complete) and explain what changes between them.
5. Let me know if the floor price is above the estimated value.

How to check: Recalculate the floor price with a calculator. Check whether the AI used only your numbers—discard any new amount that appears "out of nowhere." Test the standard option with 2 or 3 people from the conversations in Topic 3.

💡 Tip

Offering three options helps the client choose "which one" instead of "whether to buy." Make the result they’ll receive clear in the proposal—not the tool you use. "Replies to customer inquiries within 1 hour" sells better than "AI chatbot."

Why learn

A price that’s too low attracts customers who care only about price, wears you out, and makes the business impossible to sustain. Pricing methodically—with your minimum calculated, the market understood, and the value discussed—gives you confidence to negotiate and to say no to work that isn’t worth it.

Key concepts

Floor price
costs + your time
Market
the alternatives
Value
what the result is worth
Three options
basic, standard, complete

📦 Offer and delivery

A good offer fits on one screen: who it’s for, what result it delivers, what’s included, what isn’t, how long it takes, and how much it costs. Behind the scenes, delivery needs a repeatable process — the prompts that work saved, a review checklist, and a report template. That’s what turns a service into a package.

What it is

The offer is the written promise the client reads; delivery is the process that fulfills that promise every time, at the same quality. With AI, the process always includes a human review before anything reaches the client.

1 · Recipe

The prompts, templates, and steps that worked in the pilot, saved in a document. No one starts from scratch on the second delivery.

2 · Review

A fixed checklist: facts checked, names correct, client’s tone, no third-party data. AI drafts; you sign off.

3 · Proof

Before and after for the client, with their permission. One well-told real case sells more than any ad.

✓ Clear offer

  • ✓"30 product descriptions revised in 5 business days."
  • ✓Say what’s not included.
  • ✓Agree on the number of revisions.
  • ✓Be transparent about AI use.

✗ Offer that causes problems

  • ✗"AI solutions for your business."
  • ✗Unlimited revisions.
  • ✗A promise of results you can’t control, such as "doubling sales."
  • ✗Delivering AI-generated text without reading it.

Goal: write a one-screen offer and the delivery review checklist.

Write a one-page offer for one of my services.
Audience: <who>
Problem: <the pain point, in the words I heard in conversations>
Delivered result: <what the client receives>
Timeline and price: <from the three options in the previous topic>

Structure:
1. A title that states the result (without the words "revolutionary," "incredible," or "guaranteed").
2. Who it’s for and who it is NOT for.
3. What’s included and what isn’t.
4. How it works, in 3 steps.
5. One sentence saying that I use AI with human review.

Then create an 8-item REVIEW CHECKLIST for me to go through before each delivery.

How to check: Show the offer to someone in the target audience and ask them to explain, in their own words, what you sell. If they can’t say what the result is and how much it costs, the offer needs to be clearer. Check that it doesn’t promise results that depend on the client.

Why learn

AI makes it easy to produce a lot—and also makes it easy to deliver something wrong at scale. A clear scope protects you from endless requests; human review protects your reputation. Being transparent about AI use helps you avoid the worst possible conversation: the client finding out on their own.

Key concepts

Scope
what’s included and what isn’t
Recipe
repeatable process
Human review
you sign off on the delivery
Proof
real case with permission

⚖️ Risks, ethics, and the legal side

Selling something made with AI comes with responsibilities that personal use doesn’t. You’ll handle client data, deliver text and images someone will publish, and issue invoices. None of that is complicated, but all of it needs to be thought through before the first contract, not after the first problem.

🌱 New here?

LGPD is Brazil’s General Data Protection Law (Lei Geral de Proteção de Dados, Law 13.709/2018): it regulates how personal data may be collected and used. MEI (individual microentrepreneur) is the simplest way to get a CNPJ (Brazilian business registration number) and issue invoices in Brazil, but it has an income limit and a list of permitted activities that can change over time. Confirm your situation with an accountant or on the official government website.

What it is

These are the precautions for working with AI responsibly: protect clients’ data, respect rights to content and trademarks, register your business so you can charge for your work, communicate honestly, and don’t fall for or promote promises of easy money.

RiskWhat it looks likeMinimum precautions
Personal dataA client spreadsheet pasted into a free account.Anonymize, use accounts with appropriate terms, and agree in writing with the client.
Rights and trademarksAn image “in the style of” a well-known brand or character.Don’t use third-party brands, faces, or works without permission.
Incorrect informationText containing false information published by the client.A review checklist and a contract that defines responsibilities.
InformalityCharging without issuing an invoice and losing business clients.Formalize early; talk to an accountant.
Tool dependencyThe service changes its price or goes offline.Have a second tested tool (even local AI, module 1.8).

✓ Signs of a serious opportunity

  • ✓Talks about customers, problems, and work.
  • ✓Shows risks and cases that went wrong.
  • ✓Doesn’t require payment to “unlock” earnings.

✗ Signs of a scam or hype

  • ✗Guaranteed income, a short deadline, “no effort.”
  • ✗Revenue screenshots with no verification.
  • ✗The product being sold is teaching you to sell the same product.

Why learn

A data leak or an image featuring a third-party brand can cost more than your entire revenue for the year—and your reputation, which is what brings in the next client. Basic precautions are inexpensive and, besides protecting you, they become a selling point: serious clients prefer someone who takes this seriously.

🗓️ Your 30-day plan

Thirty days won’t build a business, but they’re enough to answer the question that matters: will someone pay for this? The plan below brings together everything you covered in this module over four weeks, ending with a decision. It fits alongside a job, with a few hours a week.

What it is

A four-week roadmap—problem, offer, pilot, and decision—that ends with real evidence: a pilot client served or a clear lesson about why the idea didn’t work. Both outcomes count.

Days 1–7. Choose the problem (topic 2), make your contact list, and have your first conversations (topic 3). Record everything in the table.

Days 8–14. Calculate your minimum price and three pricing options (topic 4). Write a one-screen offer and create free samples for people who showed interest.

Days 15–21. Offer the paid pilot. Deliver it using the process and checklist (topic 5). Agree in writing on the scope, timeline, and data use (topic 6).

Days 22–30. Ask for a testimonial, record the hours spent and amount received, and decide: continue as is, adjust the audience or offer, or stop and test the second problem on your list.

Goal: turn the roadmap above into a daily plan based on your available hours and your problem.

Create my 30-day plan to validate a service built with AI.
Chosen problem and audience: <from your value proposition>
Hours available per week: <hours> | Days I can work on this: <days>
Contacts I already have: <how many and where they’re from>

Plan requirements:
- 4 weeks: Problem / Offer and pricing / Pilot / Measure and decide.
- No more than 1 task per day, and each task must fit within my available hours.
- Each week ends with a verifiable deliverable (e.g., “5 conversations recorded”).
- Include a meeting with myself on day 30 with 3 questions to help me decide whether to continue, adjust, or stop.
- Don’t include revenue targets or promises of results.
Format: Day | Task | Time | Deliverable.

How to check: Add up the time for each week’s tasks and compare it with the hours you provided. Check whether the first offer for a paid pilot appears by the third week—if the plan puts the payment request off until “later,” ask to move it up. Put the tasks on your calendar today.

💡 Tip

This is the end of Track 1. Go back to the task map you made in module 1.1: many tasks have moved to a different column. If along the way you feel like building your own tool—a custom assistant with your documents, a tailored automation—Track 2 is exactly that next step.

Why learn

Knowledge without a deadline becomes an endless intention. A short plan, with weekly deliverables and a scheduled decision, turns what you’ve learned into evidence about your path—whether that’s a first client or the certainty that it’s worth testing another idea. Both matter, and neither depends on luck.
