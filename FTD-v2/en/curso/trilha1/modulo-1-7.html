🔄 Trigger and action

Every automation, from the simplest to the most elaborate, can be summed up in one sentence: “when this happens, do that”. When an email with an attachment arrives, save the attachment to a folder. When someone fills out the form, add it to the spreadsheet and let me know. When it’s Monday at 8 a.m., put together the week’s report. You already do these tasks by hand; automating them means teaching the computer to do them for you.

🌱 New here?

Trigger (trigger) is the event that starts things: a new email, a new spreadsheet row, or a time. Action is what the automation does next: create, send, copy, notify. Flow (workflow) is the sequence of a trigger and actions. No-code (no-code) means you build everything by dragging blocks onto a screen, without programming. Integration is the connection between two apps, such as Gmail and Google Sheets.

What it is

Process automation means getting apps to communicate with each other so they can carry out a sequence of steps that used to be manual, without you. It always starts with a trigger and continues with actions, sometimes with decisions along the way.

📨 Event-based

Something happened: a message, form, payment, or new file in a folder.

⏰ Scheduled

Every day at 7 a.m., every Monday, or on the last day of the month. Great for reports and reminders.

👆 Manual

You press a button and the whole sequence runs. A good way to get started and test things.

Why learn

Small, repeated tasks add up to hours each month, and they’re where you’re most likely to forget things or make typing errors. Thinking in terms of “trigger and action” helps you spot these tasks in your routine—and is the first step toward taking them off your hands.

Key concepts

Trigger
the event that starts it
Action
what the flow does
Flow
trigger + actions in order
Integration
apps that communicate

🗺️ Map it before automating

The most common mistake is opening the tool before understanding the process. Automating a mess just makes it happen faster. Before dragging the first block, write down how the task is done today, step by step, including who does it, where the information is, and what happens when something goes wrong. A simple three-step method helps: prioritize, plan, and pilot.

What it is

Mapping a process means describing each step in a recurring task—inputs, decisions, outputs, and exceptions—so you can choose what’s worth automating and design the flow before building it in the tool.

1 · Prioritize

List recurring tasks and choose one that happens often, follows clear rules, and is a pain. Save tasks that happen rarely or change every time for later.

2 · Plan

Draw the flow: trigger, steps, decisions (“if it’s urgent…”), where each piece of data lives, and what to do if something goes wrong.

3 · Pilot

Run it with test data, then alongside the manual process for a week or two, and only then let it run on its own.

QuestionGood candidatePoor candidate
How often does it happen?Every week or every day.Twice a year.
Are the steps always the same?Yes, with few exceptions.Every case is different.
Is the data in digital form?Email, form, spreadsheet.Paper, phone call, hallway conversation.
What does an error cost?Not much, and it can be fixed.A lot: money, health, contract.

Goal: describe a process and find out whether it’s worth automating.

You are a process and no-code automation consultant.
Task I do by hand: <describe it in your own words, from start to finish>
How often: <per day, week, or month>
Apps involved: <Gmail, WhatsApp, Google Sheets, store system...>

1. Ask up to 5 questions to understand the exceptions (what happens when something doesn’t go as usual).
2. Then write out the current process in numbered steps: who does each step, where the information is, and what decision is made.
3. Evaluate the frequency, clarity of the rules, whether the data is digital, and the cost of an error. Is it worth automating? Why?
4. If it is, design the flow: trigger, actions in order, decisions, and what to do if something goes wrong.
5. Point out which steps should stay with a person.

How to check: Read the numbered steps and ask yourself, “Is this really how I do it?” If the AI skipped a step or made one up, correct it before moving on. The final flow needs a clear trigger and a plan for what to do if something goes wrong.

Why learn

Half an hour of mapping can prevent days of rework in the tool. Plus, just writing down the process often helps you find unnecessary steps you can cut—sometimes the best automation is simply to stop doing something.

🧰 n8n, Make, and Zapier

The three best-known no-code automation tools are Zapier, o Make e o n8n. They all do the same basic job—connect apps with triggers and actions—and they all have ready-made blocks for using AI. They differ in how you build, where the flow runs, and how they charge. Once you learn one, you can understand the others in no time.

What it is

They’re visual platforms where each step in the flow is a block (called a step, module, or node) connected to the next one. You choose the app, connect your account, and specify which data goes from one block to another.

ZapierMaken8n
What the flow is calledZapScenarioWorkflow
How you build itA list of steps from top to bottom; the simplest way to get started.A screen with connected bubbles; good for seeing branches.A screen with connected nodes; very flexible.
Where it runsIn Zapier’s cloud.In Make’s cloud.In n8n’s cloud or on your own server.
StrengthA large catalog of ready-to-use apps.Visual flows with multiple routes.Open source, control over your data, and the option to run AI locally.
Who it’s forPeople who want quick results.People who like seeing the diagram.People who want to go further or keep data on their own devices.

🌱 New here?

Open source means anyone can download and install the software. Hosting on your own server (self-hosting) means running the software on a computer you own or rent, instead of using the company’s service. Connector is the ready-made block that knows how to communicate with an app. Credential is the permission you give the tool to access your account (for example, “you can read my Gmail”).

💡 Tip

All three offer a way to get started without paying, and charge as usage grows—each counts usage differently (tasks, operations, or runs). Plan names and prices change often; check the tool’s page on the day you sign up. The method you learn here works with any of them.

Why learn

Knowing how the tools differ helps you avoid choosing based on advertising. For most first workflows, any of them will work; the choice matters when volume, sensitive data, or a desire to run everything on your own server comes into play.

🧪 A real workflow, step by step

Let’s build a concrete workflow. Scenario: a small home renovation company receives quote requests through a form on its website. Today, someone copies each request into a spreadsheet, reads it, decides whether it’s urgent, and writes a response. The workflow below handles the busywork and leaves the final decision to a person: AI summarizes and classifies each request, the spreadsheet records it, Telegram alerts the team about urgent requests, and Gmail saves a draft —which is only sent after someone reviews it.

What it is

A six-step workflow—form, AI, spreadsheet, decision, alert, and draft—turns each quote request into an organized record and a response ready for review, without sending anything automatically.

Below is the setup in n8n. In Make and Zapier, the block names are slightly different, but the sequence is the same. Before you start, create a Google Sheets spreadsheet with these columns: date, name, contact info, service, city, summary, urgency, status.

Step 1 · Trigger. Create a new workflow and add a form trigger (in n8n, the On form submission, which creates its own form; or the Google Forms trigger in Make and Zapier). Fields: name, contact info, service, city, desired timeline, description.

Step 2 · AI. Add a language model node (OpenAI, Anthropic, Gemini, or a local model through Ollama). Connect the credential and paste the prompt from the block below, inserting the form fields into it. Ask for the response in JSON format.

Step 3 · Spreadsheet. Add a Google Sheets node with the “append row” operation. Map each column to the right field: the form fields and the ones returned by AI (summary, urgency). For status, enter “awaiting review.”

Step 4 · Decision. Add an IF node with this rule: urgency equals “high.” Connect the “true” output to Telegram.

Step 5 · Alert and draft. On the “true” output, a Telegram node sends the summary to the team group. Connected to the output of step 3 (so it runs every time, whether urgent or not), a Gmail node creates a draft (draft) for the customer’s contact address, using the text suggested by AI.

Step 6 · Test and activate. Fill out the form with a made-up request, run the workflow once, and check each node. Only then activate the workflow.

Goal: the prompt to put inside the AI node (step 2).

You triage quote requests for <company name>, which provides <type of service> in <region>.
Request received:
- Name: {{ $json.nome }}
- Service: {{ $json.servico }}
- City: {{ $json.cidade }}
- Desired timeline: {{ $json.prazo }}
- Description: {{ $json.descricao }}

Respond ONLY with valid JSON, with no text before or after it, in this format:
{
  "resumo": "up to 2 sentences describing what the customer needs",
  "urgencia": "alta" | "media" | "baixa",
  "motivo_urgencia": "1 sentence",
  "fora_da_area": true | false,
  "rascunho_resposta": "a short, friendly email confirming the request and asking for photos of the location"
}
Rules: urgency is "alta" only if there is a risk (leak, exposed wiring, water infiltration) or the requested timeline is within 3 days.
Don’t promise a price or date. If information is missing, ask for it in the draft.

How to check: Run it with three test requests—one with a leak, one with no rush, and one from another city. Check that urgency is “alta” and “baixa,” and that “fora_da_area” is true for the third. The parts between {{ }} are n8n’s way of pulling fields from the previous step—replace the names with the exact fields from your form; in Make and Zapier, you click the field instead of typing it.

💡 Tip

An AI node connected to a cloud service uses an access key from the model provider, which charges based on usage. To learn at no cost, n8n installed on your computer can connect to a local model through Ollama; the result is a little less refined, but the workflow is identical.

Why learn

Seeing a complete workflow, with a decision and human review, shows the pattern repeated in almost every useful automation: input, AI processing, record, rule, and supervised output. Once you build this one, you can adapt it for customer service, finance, or HR.

🧠 Where AI fits in the workflow

Not every step needs AI. Copying a field to a spreadsheet, comparing a date, or sending an alert are fixed rulesThey always produce the same result, cost nothing extra, and don’t make mistakes because of creativity. AI is worth it when the input is free-form, varied text — an email, a description, a complaint — that someone would need to read to understand.

What it is

It’s a step-by-step choice between fixed rules and AI. AI handles classification, data extraction, summarizing, and drafting text; fixed rules handle copying, comparing, calculating, and sending. When a mistake could be costly, a person approves it.

✓ Good tasks for AI in the workflow

  • ✓Classify messages by subject, urgency, or sentiment.
  • ✓Extract data from an email or invoice into fields.
  • ✓Summarize long texts and draft responses.

✗ Don’t let AI decide on its own

  • ✗Payments, discounts, and charges.
  • ✗Responses about health, contracts, or legal matters.
  • ✗Delete or change important records.

Why learn

Putting AI in every step makes the workflow more expensive, slower, and less predictable. Using it only where needed—and requiring human approval where errors matter—gives you the best of both worlds: the machine reads, the rule executes, and the person decides.

Key concepts

Fixed rule
same result every time
Classify
label the text
Extract
pull data from the text
Human approval
where errors matter most

🛡️ Test, monitor, and protect

An automation runs when no one is watching — and that’s exactly why it can fail silently for weeks. An account password expires, a form field changes its name, or AI returns text in the wrong format. A well-built workflow alerts you when it breaks, keeps a record of what it did, and protects the data that passes through it.

What it is

These safeguards make an automation reliable: test with fake data, get an alert when something goes wrong, check the execution history, limit AI costs, and handle personal data in accordance with the LGPD (Brazil’s data protection law).

RiskWhat it looks likeProtection
Silent failureThe workflow stopped, and no one noticed.An error workflow that sends an alert (in n8n, the Error Trigger; in Make and Zapier, failure alerts).
AI returns the wrong formatThe JSON came back malformed, so the next step fails.Request JSON only, check the fields, and send it for review if any are missing.
Loop or repetitionThe same email gets sent several times.A “status” column in the spreadsheet and a “only if not processed yet” rule.
AI costHigh volume leads to an unexpected bill.Set a spending limit in the provider’s account and use AI only for the steps that need it.
Personal dataNames, phone numbers, and CPF numbers moving through several services.Include only the necessary fields, use company accounts, and inform the customer (LGPD).

🌱 New here?

Execution is each time the workflow runs from beginning to end; the tools keep a history of these runs, including what went in and out at each step. Error workflow is a second workflow that runs only when the main one fails, to alert you. Test data is invented information (“Test Customer,” “test@example.com”) used to try things out without touching real data.

✓ Before you turn it on

  • ✓It ran with at least three different test inputs.
  • ✓Error alerts reach your phone.
  • ✓At first, messages to customers go out as drafts.

✗ Warning signs

  • ✗Testing directly with the real customer list.
  • ✗No one knows the workflow exists or how to turn it off.
  • ✗An employee’s personal password connected to the workflow.

Why learn

An automation without monitoring becomes a hidden risk: it can stop working, send the wrong message, or expose data. These safeguards take little time and make it possible to trust the workflow and let it run for real.

Key concepts

Test data
try it without risk
Error workflow
alerts you when it breaks
History
what each run did
Minimum data
only what’s needed (LGPD)

🛠️ Your first automation

The best first automation is small, frequent, and low-risk. Don’t start with the company’s most important process; start with something that’s annoying every week and that you can fix in two minutes if it goes wrong. The five ideas below cover the most common situations for people just getting started.

What it is

An exercise to choose a simple automation, ask the assistant for the complete workflow specification, and build it in the tool you chose, following the module’s method.

📎 Organized attachments

Email with an attachment from a sender → save it in the right Drive folder, with the date in the filename.

🎯 Qualified contacts

Website form → AI classifies the interest → leads spreadsheet with priority.

📊 Weekly report

Every Monday at 8 a.m. → reads the sales spreadsheet → AI writes the summary → sends it by email.

💬 Drafted responses

Customer email → AI identifies the subject → response draft for you to review.

🚨 Useful alert

Negative review or request above a certain amount → immediate alert on Telegram.

✍️ Yours

The task you mapped in Topic 2, if it passed the good-candidate test.

Goal: receive the step-by-step specification for your first automation, in the tool you chose.

I want to build my first no-code automation.
Tool: <n8n, Make, or Zapier>
Automation: <choose one of the ideas or describe your own>
Apps I use: <Gmail, Google Drive, Google Sheets, Telegram...>
My level: I’ve never built an automation.

Give me:
1. A step-by-step flow diagram (trigger, actions, decisions).
2. For each step: the block name in that tool, what to configure, and which fields to connect.
   If you’re unsure of a block’s exact name, say so and describe what to look for.
3. Where AI fits (if it does) and the prompt for that step, with a JSON response.
4. Three test inputs and the expected result for each.
5. How to configure an error alert.
6. What NOT to automate in this flow and should stay with a person.

How to check: Open the tool and look for each block mentioned. If one doesn’t exist under that name, ask the assistant, “What’s the current equivalent of X in <tool>?” or check the official documentation—the tools rename blocks over time. Run the three tests and compare the results with what you expected before activating the flow.

💡 Tip

Keep a simple spreadsheet of every automation you create: its name, what it does, the tool, who is responsible, and how to turn it off. Once you have five or six flows, this list becomes a map that helps prevent surprises—and shows how much repetitive work you’ve taken out of your routine.

Why learn

Getting your first automation working changes how you see work: every repeated task becomes a candidate. Starting small helps make that first experience a success instead of a shock.
