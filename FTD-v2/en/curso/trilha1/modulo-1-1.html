🎯 AI without the mystery

Artificial intelligence is the name given to programs that perform tasks that once required a person to think: understanding a written request, recognizing a face in a photo, summarizing a contract, writing an email. The difference from an ordinary program is where the rules come from. In an ordinary program, someone writes each rule by hand. In modern AI, the program learns the rules on its own, by looking at millions of examples.

🌱 New here?

Algorithm is a recipe of steps that a computer follows. Model is the result of training: a huge file of numbers that stores learned patterns. Train means adjusting those numbers until the model gets the examples right. When you use ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini, you’re talking to a model that has already been trained.

What it is

AI is a broad field. Machine learning is part of it; it learns from data. Deep learning is part of machine learning; it uses neural networks with many layers. Language models, which you use every day, are part of deep learning.

✓ AI is

  • ✓A very good pattern recognizer, trained on examples.
  • ✓A tool that improves when you give it context.
  • ✓Fast at drafting, summarizing, brainstorming, and writing code.

✗ AI is not

  • ✗An encyclopedia that’s always right.
  • ✗A person with intentions or feelings.
  • ✗Responsible for decisions—you are the one who decides.

Why learn

When you understand that AI is a pattern recognizer, you know when to trust it and when to check its work. If you think it’s magic, you swing between fear and blind trust—and both can be costly.

Training

Deep learning

📜 From 1950 to agents

AI didn’t begin with ChatGPT. The idea is more than seventy years old and has gone through “summers” (years of excitement and investment) and “winters” (years when promises went unfulfilled and investment dried up). Knowing about these ups and downs protects you from hype: every wave arrives promising too much, and what lasts is what solves a real problem.

What it is

The history of AI is a series of leaps: hand-written rules, then learning from data, then deep networks, then language models, and now agents that carry out tasks.

1950 · Turing Test — Alan Turing asks whether a machine can converse well enough that it can’t be distinguished from a person.

1956 · Dartmouth — a gathering of researchers coins the term “artificial intelligence.”

1970s–80s · Expert systems — rules written by hand by experts; they work in specialized areas and break down outside them.

1997 · Deep Blue — an IBM computer beats the world chess champion.

2012 · AlexNet — a deep neural network takes the lead in image recognition; the deep learning era begins.

2017 · Transformer — the architecture behind virtually all current language models.

2022 · ChatGPT — generative AI reaches the general public through chat.

2024–2026 · Reasoning and agents — models that “think before they answer” and agents that use tools, work with files, and spend hours on a task.

💡 Tip

When you read a headline saying “AI will replace everything in six months,” remember the winters. Ask: What specific task does this solve today, and who is already using it?

Why learn

The timeline shows that today’s leap came from three things coming together: lots of data, lots of computing power, and a better architecture (the Transformer). Knowing this helps you anticipate what’s next and separate real progress from hype.

Key concepts

AI winter
a promise that went unfulfilled
Expert system

Transformer

Agent

🔮 How an LLM works

A LLM (from Large Language Model, meaning a large language model) does one thing, over and over, very quickly: predicts the next piece of text. It reads what you wrote, calculates the most likely continuations, chooses one, adds it to the text, and repeats. A three-paragraph answer is the result of doing this hundreds of times.

🌱 New here?

Token is a piece of text the model sees—a short word, part of a word, or a symbol. “Intelligence” might become two or three tokens. Context window is how much text the model can consider at once: the entire conversation, the files you pasted, and its own response. Parameters are the numbers adjusted during training; large models have billions of them.

What it is

An LLM is a model trained on a huge amount of text to predict the next token. It then goes through a second round of training with examples of good answers and human feedback, turning it into an assistant that follows instructions.

1 · Pretraining

It reads a mountain of public text and learns to predict the next token. The result is a model that completes text but doesn’t have conversations.

2 · Fine-tuning

It trains on examples of questions and good answers. It learns the assistant format.

3 · Preferences

People and other models evaluate responses; the model is adjusted to prefer useful, honest, and safe ones.

Why learn

Understanding that the model predicts text explains almost everything you’ll see: why context improves the answer, why it confidently makes things up, and why asking for a clear format works. It’s the mental model that guides the rest of the course.

Key concepts

Token
piece of text
Context window
what fits at once
Probability
chance of each continuation
Pretraining

🧩 What AI can already create

Generative AI is AI that creates something new instead of just classifying what already exists. The same “predict the next piece” logic applies to image pixels, audio segments, and video frames. And today’s models are increasingly multimodal: they understand and produce different types of content in the same conversation.

🌱 New here?

Multimodal means “in multiple modes”: the same model can receive a photo and text, or listen to your voice and respond aloud. Prompt is the request you write for AI—the whole subject of Module 1.3.

What it is

Generative AI produces text, images, audio, video, and code from a request. Each type of output has its own tools and considerations, but the principle is the same: the clearer the request, the better the result.

✍️ Text

Emails, summaries, scripts, translations, analysis of long documents.

🖼️ Image

Illustrations, mockups, product photos, editing through instructions. Module 1.5.

🎙️ Voice

Transcription, narration, real-time voice conversations.

🎬 Video

Short clips from text or images, talking avatars.

💻 Code

Scripts, websites, automations; agents that program entire projects.

📊 Data

Reading spreadsheets, finding patterns, generating charts and reports.

💡 Tip

Start with the type of output you produce most at work. If you write a lot, start with text; if you make posts, start with images. Building confidence with one type helps you learn the others.

Why learn

Knowing what’s already possible helps you avoid two mistakes: spending hours doing by hand what AI can do in minutes, and expecting it to do things it still can’t do well. This is the map you’ll explore in Modules 1.3 through 1.7.

💼 What changes at work

AI doesn’t arrive and replace entire professions all at once: it enters through tasks. A job is a bundle of tasks—some repetitive and predictable, others requiring judgment, relationships, and accountability. AI takes on the first group sooner; the second stays with you and becomes more important.

What it is

It’s a shift in who does each task: AI takes on drafting, searching, formatting, and repetition; people handle direction, verification, decisions, and relationships.

✓ Delegate to AI

  • ✓First drafts of texts and presentations.
  • ✓Summaries of meetings, documents, and long emails.
  • ✓Formatting, translation, and Portuguese proofreading.
  • ✓Initial research with sources for you to check.

✗ Don’t outsource

  • ✗The final decision about clients, contracts, and people.
  • ✗Relationships: trust is built in person.
  • ✗Checking numbers, names, and facts.
  • ✗Understanding your own business.

Why learn

Looking at tasks takes away the abstract fear (“Will I lose my job?”) and brings up a practical question: which of my tasks can AI already do well, and what will I do with the time I save? The people who answer this first get ahead.

Key concepts

Task × profession
AI enters through the task
Delegation
moves up one step at a time
Verification
increases with autonomy
Judgment

⚠️ Where AI gets it wrong

Because the model chooses the most likely continuation, it sometimes chooses a continuation that’s plausible but false —and writes with the same confidence it has when it gets things right. This is called a hallucination. It’s more common with numbers, dates, quotations, names of laws, and bibliographic references.

What it is

These are known AI limitations: making up facts (hallucination), not knowing what happened after training (cutoff date), repeating biases in the data (bias), and exposing what you pasted into the conversation (privacy).

LimitationWhat it looks likeHow to protect yourself
HallucinationCites a law, book, or number that doesn’t exist.Ask for the source and check the original.
Cutoff dateDoesn’t know recent facts or gives outdated prices.Use the tool’s web search or paste in current information.
BiasStereotypes in text and images.Review critically and ask for alternatives.
PrivacyCustomer data pasted into a service without a contract.Anonymize it and use accounts with appropriate terms.

🌱 New here?

Cutoff date is the last day of data the model saw during training. Anything after that, it only knows if the tool searches the web or you paste in the information. Anonymizing means replacing names, ID numbers, and contact details with placeholders (Client A, CPF-X) before you paste them in.

Try this: make the AI show what it knows and what it doesn’t know.

I want to understand a topic without getting caught up in made-up information.
Topic: <the topic you want to study>

1. Explain the topic in 5 short points.
2. Mark every claim with a number, date, or proper name with [VERIFY].
3. At the end, list what you are NOT sure about and tell me where I can confirm it (type of source, not made-up links).
4. State your knowledge cutoff date and what may have changed since then.

How to check: Take two items marked [VERIFY] and look them up in an official source. If either one doesn’t check out, you’ve just seen a hallucination happen—and learned how to catch one.

Why learn

Using AI without knowing its limits is like driving without knowing where the brakes are. The habit of checking facts, anonymizing data, and questioning overly confident answers is what separates people who use AI safely from those who embarrass themselves.

🛠️ Your first real use

Enough theory. The best first use of AI isn’t asking for a poem: it’s asking for help seeing your own work. In 10 minutes, you’ll put together a task map showing where AI can help right away, where it can help with care, and where it shouldn’t be used.

What it is

A guided exercise: you describe your routine, the AI organizes tasks by their potential uses, and you choose one to test this week.

Step 1. Open the assistant you use (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, or another one) in a new conversation.

Step 2. Paste the prompt below, replacing what’s inside < >.

Step 3. Answer the questions it asks—that’s what makes the map useful.

Step 4. Choose ONE task from the “right away” column and use AI for it in the next few days.

Goal: create a task map for your work.

You are a productivity consultant who understands generative AI.
My job: <your role, for example: owner of an online clothing store>
My typical week: <list 8 to 15 tasks you do, one per line>

Before responding, ask me up to 3 questions to better understand my routine.
Then organize the tasks in a table with these columns:
Task | AI can help RIGHT AWAY | AI can help WITH CARE | DO NOT delegate | Why | First test in 15 minutes
Finish by naming the ONE task with the best effort-to-benefit ratio for me to start with.

How to check: the table should include every task you listed, each in just one column, and a “first test” you can do today. If any task is missing or the test is vague, ask: “redo it with every task included and concrete tests.”

💡 Tip

Save this map. When you finish Track 1, do the exercise again: several tasks will have moved to a different column because you’ll know how to write better prompts (1.3), delegate to agents (1.4), and automate (1.7).

Why learn

Just reading about AI doesn’t make what you learn stick. Using it on one of your real tasks, with a short test and a check, turns a concept into a habit—and shows you in practice what this module explained.
