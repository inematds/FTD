MODULE 3.8

🧭 AI Leadership and Strategy

Technology is no longer the bottleneck. What separates companies that achieve results from those that accumulate pilots is making good decisions about where AI fits, measuring honestly, building the right team, and guiding people through change. This module gives leaders decision-making tools—worksheets, matrices, logs, and checklists.

🎯 Problem before AI

The question “what can we do with AI?” produces long lists and short results. The question that works is the reverse: “which business outcomes are stuck, and which tasks are holding them up?”. AI strategy is business strategy applied to a new set of capabilities—reading, writing, classifying, conversing, and taking action in systems—not a separate technology plan.

The path has five steps: a goal business goal, the process that drives it, the tasks tasks in that process, the use case (where AI enters a task), and the metric that tells you whether it worked. Skipping steps is why most pilots “worked technically” but changed nothing.

What it is

Derive each AI initiative from an explicit business goal, moving through the process and task to a metric measured before and after—instead of starting with a tool and looking for somewhere to use it.

✓ Good questions to start with

  • ✓Where is the workload growing faster than the team?
  • ✓Which decisions wait days for analysis that could take hours?
  • ✓What knowledge is stuck in the heads of a few people?

✗ Starting points that often fail

  • ✗“Our competitor launched a chatbot; we need one.”
  • ✗“We bought licenses for everyone; now figure out what to do.”
  • ✗Choosing the model before knowing what task it will perform.

Why learn

Leaders are held accountable for results, not technology adoption. Anchoring each initiative to a goal and a metric makes it possible to have a conversation with leadership, protects the budget for cases that work, and gives you a reason to say “no”—perhaps the most valuable decision an AI leader makes.

Key concepts

Business goal
the starting point
Use case
AI in a specific task
Metric
proof that it worked
Saying no
focus is a choice
2

🗂️ Use case portfolio

Once the ideas come in, the leader’s job is to treat them as a portfolio: a set of bets with different levels of value, feasibility, and risk, reviewed at fixed intervals. No single case defines the strategy; the balance among them does. A healthy portfolio combines quick wins that build confidence with a few larger bets that require data preparation.

Feasibility in AI has three parts: Does the data exist and can you access it? Can the task tolerate occasional errors with review? Is integration with the systems straightforward? Risk Risk increases when personal data is involved, when decisions affect people (credit, hiring, health), or when customers see the response without review.

What it is

AI portfolio management means keeping all use cases on the same scorecard—value, feasibility, risk, cost, and status—and periodically deciding what to add, scale, or close.

Goal: a standard case sheet for every use case in the portfolio.

# use-case.yaml — one sheet per portfolio case
id: CU-<number>
name: <e.g., after-sales ticket triage>
business_owner: <name and title — who is accountable for the outcome>
technical_owner: <team responsible for operations>
problem: <what hurts today, in one sentence, without mentioning AI>
primary_metric: <e.g., time to first response>
baseline: <value measured today, with date and source>
target: <expected value and timeframe>
value: <1 to 5>          # business impact if successful
feasibility: <1 to 5>    # data ready, errors tolerable, simple integration
risk: <low | medium | high>  # personal data? decisions about people? customer sees it?
data: <sources; any personal data? what is the legal basis under the LGPD?>
estimated_cost: <implementation range + monthly operating cost>
stop_criterion: <what, measured by when, ends the case>
status: <idea | pilot | production | closed>
next_review: <yyyy-mm-dd>

How to check: Give the sheet to someone outside the project and ask: “Three months from now, would you know whether it worked?” If the answer is no, you’re missing a metric, baseline, or stop criterion. Rule of thumb: a case without a measured baseline stays at “idea” status.

Why learn

Without a portfolio, the AI agenda is set by whoever shouts the loudest or by the most persistent vendor. With one consistent yardstick and regular reviews, you allocate people and money where there are returns, end pilots without drama, and show leadership the big picture instead of a list of experiments.

Key concepts

Portfolio
balanced bets
Feasibility
data, error, integration
Baseline
the measured before
Stop criterion
when to end it

📊 Honest ROI

A typical AI business case has two symmetrical flaws: it underestimates costs (counting only licenses or tokens) and overestimates benefits (multiplying “minutes saved per person” by salary and calling it profit). Honest ROI count the full cost and call something a gain only when it shows up in a business metric.

The most useful distinction is among three types of benefits. Cash: new revenue or a cost that goes away. Capacity: time freed up— which only turns into results if you reallocate it to something that creates value. Risk: losses avoided, such as errors, fines, or delays. Time freed up that does not change any allocation decision is not ROI; it is convenience.

New term

Counterfactual is what would have happened without the initiative—the right comparison, which is rarely “nothing would change.” Comparison group is a part of the team that continues without the tool during the pilot, so you can separate AI’s effect from other changes during that period.

What it is

It means calculating the return on an AI initiative using all costs (direct and hidden), benefits classified as cash, capacity, or risk, a comparison with the realistic scenario without AI, and a pilot designed to confirm or disprove the hypothesis.

CostUsually included?Why it matters
Licenses and tokensYesVaries with usage; grows with adoption.
Systems integrationSometimesIt is often the largest part of the engineering effort.
Human reviewRarelyThe people who check the answers still get paid.
Evaluation and maintenanceRarelyPrompts, indexes, and tests need a permanent owner.
Change management and trainingAlmost neverWithout it, the tool goes unused and the benefit never appears.

Try this: put your business case in front of a “skeptical CFO.”

Act as a skeptical CFO reviewing an AI business case.
Case: <description in 3 to 5 lines>
Costs I estimated: <list with amounts and assumptions>
Benefits I estimated: <list, saying how each one was measured>
Baseline: <current metrics, with date and source>

1. List costs that are often missed and do not appear on my list
   (integration, human review, training, prompt and evaluation maintenance,
   model changes, managers’ time, idle infrastructure).
2. Classify each benefit as CASH, CAPACITY, or RISK. For each
   CAPACITY benefit, say what allocation decision needs to happen for it to become a result.
3. Point out where I compare against an unrealistic “no AI” scenario.
4. Build 3 scenarios (pessimistic, likely, optimistic) using ONLY my numbers.
   Where a number is missing, write “needs measuring”—do not make up a market benchmark.
5. Propose a pilot lasting up to <time frame> with a comparison group that confirms
   or disproves the main hypothesis, and say what result would make me give up.

How to check: look through the response for any number you did not provide—if there is one, it is made up and should be removed. A good result leaves you with a “needs measuring” list and a pilot with a clear stopping criterion. Take that list, not the optimistic spreadsheet, to the approval meeting.

Why learn

Inflated promises win the first budget and cost you the second. A leader who presents full costs and classified benefits earns credibility with finance — and that credibility is what funds bigger bets later.

Key concepts

Full cost
beyond licenses and tokens
Cash · capacity · risk
three types of benefits
Counterfactual
the realistic scenario without AI
Comparison group
isolate the effect

🔀 Build or buy

“Build or buy” in AI has five positions, not two. At one end, you buy a ready-made product (an office assistant, a customer service tool) and just configure it. At the other, you train your own model—a rare option that requires data and scale few companies have. In between are the more common options: configure a platform with your data, build your product on a model API and fine-tune an open model (the subject of module 3.2).

The main criterion is differentiation: if the capability is the same for everyone in the market (summarizing meetings, proofreading Portuguese), buy it. If it depends on your process, your data, or your accumulated knowledge—and that is why customers choose you—build the layer that sets you apart, usually on top of a third-party model through the gateway. Many companies do both for different use cases.

What it is

It means choosing, case by case, a position on the spectrum between buying a ready-made solution and training from scratch, weighing differentiation, the role of your own data, time to value, total cost, data control, and how easily you can switch vendors.

Goal: Record the build-or-buy decision in an ADR (architecture decision record).

# ADR-<no.>: <buy | configure | build | customize> for <use case>
Date: <yyyy-mm-dd>    Status: proposed | accepted | superseded by ADR-<no.>

## Context
- Problem and metric: <...>
- Constraints: <LGPD, regulated industry, data residency, timeline, budget, team>

## Options considered
1. <Buy product X>
2. <Build on an API, through an internal gateway>
3. <Customize open model Y and run it on your own infrastructure>

## Criteria (score each option from 1 to 5)
| criterion                             | opt. 1 | opt. 2 | opt. 3 |
|---------------------------------------|--------|--------|--------|
| Is this a differentiator for customers? |        |        |        |
| Do your own data change the result?    |        |        |        |
| Time to first value                    |        |        |        |
| Total cost over 3 years                 |        |        |        |
| Data control and residency              |        |        |        |
| Ease of switching (lock-in)             |        |        |        |
| Team’s ability to maintain it           |        |        |        |

## Decision
<option>, because <two or three sentences>.

## Consequences
- Gets easier: <...>
- Gets harder: <...>

## Review trigger
<event or date that reopens this decision—for example, a price change or an open model reaching the required quality>

How to check: A good ADR lets someone who joins the company later understand why the decision was made and when it should be reviewed. If the “Options” section has only one real option, the decision was made before the analysis—go back and include at least one serious alternative.

💡 Tip

When you buy, negotiate three things upfront that can matter later: export of your data and configurations in an open format, a clause about using your data to train the vendor’s models, and advance notice of model or price changes. These determine the cost of switching.

Why learn

Building something you could buy ties up your team in work that doesn’t set you apart; buying something you should build hands your competitive advantage to a vendor. Making the decision along the spectrum and documenting it avoids both mistakes and makes it possible to revisit the decision when the market changes—which happens every few months in AI.

Key concepts

Differentiation
what makes customers choose you
Lock-in
cost of switching vendors
ADR
documented, reviewable decision
Review trigger

👥 Team and roles

The 2026 AI team looks different from the data science team of a few years ago. Since most use cases rely on ready-made models, the work has shifted from training models to integrating, evaluating, and operating them. The most sought-after role is often the AI applications engineer: someone who knows software, knows how to provide the model with context and tools, and knows how to measure quality.

Some nontechnical roles also make a difference: the domain expert, who defines what a good answer looks like and writes the test cases; the product owner, who decides what gets built; and legal and security, involved from the design stage, not just for final approval.

What it is

This is the design of the roles needed to bring AI into production—applications engineering, platform, data, evaluation, product, domain expertise, security, and legal—and how to organize them: centrally, distributed across teams, or in a hybrid model.

Organizational modelWorks whenRisk
CentralizedThe company is getting started and needs standards and a small group of specialists working together.It becomes a bottleneck and gets distant from the teams’ real problems.
Distributed across teamsTeams already have technical maturity and a shared platform is in place.Each team reinvents the wheel; standards and security practices diverge.
Hybrid (center of excellence + teams)A core team handles the platform, standards, and training; teams build the use cases.It requires clarity about who decides what, or it turns into conflict.

✓ When hiring and training

  • ✓Test with a real business problem, not trivia questions.
  • ✓Value people who measure quality and write evaluations, not just those who give demos.
  • ✓Train people from within who know the process; teaching AI is usually faster than teaching the business.

✗ Common mistakes

  • ✗Hiring researchers for work that involves integration and operations.
  • ✗Leaving out the domain expert and discovering the mistakes in production.
  • ✗An “AI team” with no product owner: lots of proof of concept, little product.

Why learn

Team structure determines what the company can deliver. A poor setup—research only, centralized only, or no domain expertise—holds back the entire portfolio, no matter how good the strategy is. Getting the roles and organization right is the cheapest way to speed up all use cases at once.

🧠 Change culture and management

The phrase that most often blocks adoption isn’t technical: “Is this going to take my job?” When leadership doesn’t answer, each person answers on their own, and the most common response is to resist quietly—use it sparingly, point out flaws, and not share what works. Transparency about what will change is the first step, including when the answer is “some roles will change a lot, and here’s how we’ll handle that.”

A well-known model for organizing individual change is ADKAR, from the consulting firm Prosci: awareness (why change), desire (what I gain), knowledge (how to do it), ability (actually doing it at work), and reinforcement (not going back to the old way). Its practical value is diagnostic: when a department doesn’t adopt, find out which step it got stuck on.

What it is

AI change management means guiding people from the old way of working to the new one, with transparency about the impact, hands-on training, aligned incentives, and reinforcement—treating resistance as information about where the process fell short.

Step 1 · Explain what will change. For each affected role: which tasks will go away, which will be added, and what will happen to the people doing that work today.

Step 2 · Align incentives. If the target is based on hours worked, no one wants to get faster. Review targets and recognize people who share what worked.

Step 3 · Train with real work. Exercises using tasks from people’s actual roles, not generic demos.

Step 4 · Lead by example. Managers who use it and show how they use it—including the mistakes they caught—give others permission to experiment.

💡 Tip

Create a safe space to make mistakes with AI: a channel where people can share what they tried, what went wrong, and what they learned. Hidden mistakes get repeated; shared mistakes become company practice.

Why learn

The technology may be ready while the company isn’t. AI returns depend on hundreds of people changing small habits, and that doesn’t happen by decree. Leaders who treat change as management work, with a method and measurable results, turn licenses into outcomes.

Key concepts

ADKAR
five steps of change
Transparency
explain what will change
Aligned incentives
a target that rewards the new way
Leadership by example

🛡️ Governance and decision-making cadence

AI governance has a reputation for being bureaucratic, so many companies either skip it or do it only on paper. The version that works is proportionate to the risk: an internal meeting summary follows a simple process; a system that influences credit, hiring, or health goes through a full review, with a documented assessment and defined human oversight.

There are established references to help you avoid starting from scratch: the NIST AI RMF (the U.S. AI risk management framework), the ISO/IEC 42001 (AI management system, certifiable), and the European AI Act, which classifies systems by risk level and applies to those serving the European market. In Brazil, the LGPD (Brazil’s data protection law) already applies to any AI that processes personal data, and a specific legal framework for AI is under discussion in Congress—track its progress, because the obligations depend on the final text.

What it is

It’s the minimum set of rules, roles, and routines that determines which AI uses are allowed, what controls apply at each risk level, who approves them, and when the full portfolio is reviewed—with a fixed cadence, not only when there’s a crisis.

🟢 Low risk

Internal use, no personal data, human reviews everything. Approval by the case owner; record it in the catalog.

🟡 Medium risk

Personal data or customers see the output. Documented assessment, privacy review, ongoing monitoring.

🔴 High risk

Decisions about people or regulated areas. Committee review, impact assessment, defined human oversight, periodic audit.

Goal: fixed agenda for the quarterly AI portfolio review.

QUARTERLY AI PORTFOLIO REVIEW — <quarter/year>
Participants: <sponsor, case owners, platform, legal/DPO, security>

[ ] Each active case has a business owner, metric, baseline, and current value
[ ] For cases stalled for two reviews: decide whether to scale, adjust, or close
[ ] Cost per result for each case compared with the previous quarter
[ ] Incidents during the period (quality, security, data) and what changed as a result
[ ] Model and vendor inventory updated; data use terms reviewed
[ ] High-risk cases have documented human oversight and assessments
[ ] Regulatory changes: LGPD/ANPD, AI legal framework, industry rules
[ ] Short user survey: actual use, trust, what gets in the way
[ ] Training: who still hasn’t completed the track for their role
[ ] Build-or-buy decisions where the review trigger has been reached (ADRs)

Required output: up to 3 decisions, each with an owner and date.

How to check: The meeting worked if it ended with documented decisions (including a decision to end something), not just presentations. At the next review, the first agenda item is to check whether the previous decisions were carried out. If no case has been closed in a year, question the stopping criterion.

💡 Tip

Write the AI use policy on one page, in plain language: what’s allowed, what isn’t, and where to ask for help. A thirty-page policy won’t be read; a one-page policy will be remembered.

Why learn

Without governance, the first serious incident leads to a blanket ban and years of delay. With governance that matches the risk and a fixed cadence, the company can experiment quickly in low-risk areas and carefully in high-risk ones—and show regulators, customers, and the board that it knows what it’s doing.

Key concepts

Proportional risk
controls matched to impact
ISO/IEC 42001
AI management system
Human oversight
who decides and when
Cadence
