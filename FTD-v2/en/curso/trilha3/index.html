🏛️ Large-Scale AI Architecture

Model routing, inference, batching, semantic caching, multi-region architecture, and cost limits at scale.

What it is:

What is AI architecture at scale? It’s the set of layers between applications and models: a gateway that identifies, limits, and logs; a router that chooses the right model; inference servers or APIs; caches; and observability that measures…

Why learn it:

Without the map, every scaling problem becomes an isolated fire: one team adds caching, another adds retries, a third switches models. With the map, you decide which layer should solve each problem once for the whole company—and you can switch models without…

Key concepts:

Gateway the single entry point for models p95 latency the time for the fastest 95% Throughput work delivered per second Observability measure before you optimize

What it is:

What is model routing? It’s the layer that chooses, for each call, the lowest-cost model capable of meeting the required quality, and redirects the call to another model or provider if the first one fails.

Why learn it:

Routing is the cost lever with the greatest impact and the lowest risk: you don’t change the product; you just stop paying frontier-model prices for simple tasks. And fallback turns a provider outage—which currently takes the product down—into a temporary increase in…

Key concepts:

Router chooses the model for each call Cascade try the cheap model first, then the expensive one if it fails Validator objective test of the output Fallback alternate route when something fails

What it is:

What is it? It’s the engineering of serving models: grouping requests, managing context memory, choosing quantization and hardware, and deciding between a managed API and your own server, while measuring TTFT, tokens per second, and cost per million tokens.

Why learn it:

The “API or your own server” decision is one of the most expensive mistakes to make in AI. Understanding prefill, decode, batching, and KV cache helps you critically assess a vendor benchmark and calculate the right costs—and understand why long prompts make everything more expensive and slower.

Key concepts:

TTFT time until the first token Continuous batching an open slot gets a new request KV cache memory of what’s already been read Quantization smaller weights, less memory

What it is:

What is prompt caching? It reuses the processing of a repeated prefix while generating a new response. Semantic caching reuses the entire response to a similar question, based on embedding similarity and a calibrated threshold.

Why learn it:

The two caches reduce costs in different ways: prompt caching makes the long context repeated in every call cheaper; semantic caching eliminates entire calls. Knowing which one to use—and how to order the prompt so prompt caching works—often saves…

Key concepts:

Prompt cache reused fixed prefix Embedding meaning represented as a vector Threshold how similar is close enough TTL how long the response stays valid

What it is:

What is multi-region resilience? It means keeping the AI service running when a provider, model, or region fails: approved alternatives, health checks, a circuit breaker, replicas of context data, and a planned degraded mode.

Why learn it:

The more AI becomes part of critical processes, the more a provider outage becomes a business outage. A resilience plan written before an incident—with approved routes, defined RTO and RPO, and a tested degraded mode—is what separates an hour of slowdown from a…

Key concepts:

Circuit breaker stops retrying what’s failing RTO / RPO downtime / data lost Data residency where data can go Degraded mode fewer features, no shutdown

What it is:

What are cost limits? They’re rules applied before each call—rate, size, budget per tenant, and agent steps—with the actual cost logged afterward, so spending is predictable, attributable, and automatically cut off when it exceeds…

Why learn it:

Managers approve AI at scale when costs are predictable. With a budget per tenant, automatic degradation, and costs attributed to each product feature, the conversation shifts from “the bill came in high” to “this feature costs this much per user and is worth it”—…

Key concepts:

Tenant who has a separate budget Rate limit calls per minute Degradation cheaper model at the limit AI FinOps measure, attribute, control

What it is:

What is it? A seven-step review guide, from metrics to cost limits, plus an analysis prompt that turns your system description into a prioritized list of decisions, each with a way to measure it.

Why learn it:

Technical leaders rarely design systems alone; they lead reviews. A fixed guide ensures no layer is overlooked, and the analysis prompt speeds up the first draft without replacing real measurement with the model’s opinion.

Key concepts:

Architecture review questions for each layer Measure before optimizing optimization without metrics is guesswork Prioritization effort × impact Formula, not guesswork numbers with sources

🧬 Custom and Proprietary Models

Continuous pretraining, distillation, open models, proprietary data as an advantage, and the real cost of maintaining a model.

What it is:

What it is The customization ladder ranks ways to adapt AI to your business, from the cheapest and most reversible (prompt) to the most expensive and permanent (a model built from scratch). Each rung solves a different kind of problem: prompts and RAG change what the model knows at the time…

Why learn it:

The question "Do we need our own model?" comes up in every planning process. With the ladder, you replace an opinion with a diagnosis: which symptom, which rung, and which test proves that the rung below isn’t enough. This prevents six-month projects that a…

Key concepts:

Rung level of customization Knowledge × behavior RAG × fine-tuning Reversibility how much it costs to undo Proof of need evaluation before moving up

What it is:

What it is An open model is a model whose weights you can download, run on your own infrastructure, and adjust. It’s the foundation of almost every corporate proprietary model: you inherit general capabilities and add what’s specific to your business.

Why learn it:

Choosing the base model sets the ceiling for your customized model, the hardware you’ll need, and the legal risk. Switching bases later means redoing fine-tuning, evaluation, and integration. Making the decision with written criteria saves months.

Key concepts:

Open weights download and run wherever you want License what you’re allowed to do Quantization fewer bits, less memory Base model the ceiling for what you can adjust

What it is:

What it is Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) trains a minimal fraction of the parameters to change the model’s behavior—format, tone, classification, tool use—without the cost of training everything. Multiple adapters can share the same…

Why learn it:

LoRA is the customization rung with the best cost-benefit ratio when the problem is behavior. Knowing that it depends almost entirely on data quality and cleanliness changes where the technical lead assigns the team: to curation, not to searching for…

Key concepts:

Adapter small file added to the base QLoRA LoRA on a 4-bit base Curation a few good examples Evaluation split data the model doesn’t see during training

What it is:

What it is Continue pretraining an open model with a large corpus from a specific domain, followed by instruction tuning and dual evaluation: gains in the domain and preservation of general capabilities.

Why learn it:

This is the rung that separates adaptation from true specialization. For domains with highly specialized language, it may be the only path to high quality—but it requires a volume of data, GPUs, and evaluation discipline that few companies have. Knowing this helps you say…

Key concepts:

Corpus raw text from the domain Catastrophic forgetting losing what it already knew Data mix domain + general Dual evaluation gains and preservation

What it is:

What it is Distillation transfers the ability of a large model, for a defined task, to a small model that costs less per call and responds faster—trading generality for efficiency.

Why learn it:

Distillation is the most direct way to reduce the unit cost of AI at high volumes without losing much quality on the task that matters. It’s also a common legal trap. A leader who knows both sides can approve the right project with the right teacher model.

Key concepts:

Teacher large reference model Student trained small model Synthetic data generated by a model Terms of use may prohibit distillation

What it is:

What it is A data moat is a competitive advantage built on exclusive data that can legally be used and is continuously renewed through product use, combined with an in-house evaluation that shows whether each model version is actually better.

Why learn it:

Investing in your own model without a data moat means buying an asset that depreciates with every release. Investing in the data cycle and your own eval creates an asset that grows in value and works with any future base model—including when you switch providers without…

Key concepts:

Moat an advantage that’s hard to copy Flywheel usage that drives improvement In-house eval what “good” means for your business Legal basis LGPD before training

What it is:

What it is The total of everything an in-house model requires over its useful life—infrastructure, data, evaluation, retraining, security, compliance, and team—compared with the cost of using and adapting a third-party model.

Why learn it:

Decisions to build your own model are among the most expensive AI decisions to reverse. Leaders who bring the full TCO, a completed checklist, and a reassessment criterion to the committee approve fewer projects—and the ones they approve deliver returns.

Key concepts:

TCO total cost of ownership Opportunity cost the market doesn’t stop Build × buy decision with a checklist Reassessment with each new base model

🎞️ Advanced Multimodal AI

How multimodal models work, real-time voice, video, complex documents, and agents using computers.

What it is:

What it is: A multimodal model is an LLM that takes in (and sometimes produces) more than one type of data. Images, audio, and video are converted into tokens by specialized encoders and enter the same context window as text—taking up space and costing as much as text.

Why learn it:

Knowing that an image becomes tokens explains cost, latency, and limits—why tiny text in a large photo fails, why ten scanned pages fill up the context window—and guides the choice between a native frontier model and an open, connected model that runs inside the…

Key concepts:

Visual encoder pixels into vectors Projection bridge to the LLM Image tokens take up context and cost money Native × connected quality × adaptation

What it is:

What it is: Structured document extraction: an image or PDF goes in, and validated JSON with known fields comes out. The model reads the layout; your code checks the format, totals, and consistency before saving.

Why learn it:

Document extraction is by far one of the multimodal use cases with the easiest returns to measure: hours of data entry become minutes of review. But only if the architecture includes a schema, validation, and review—without those, silent errors enter the accounting system.

Key concepts:

OCR text without layout Visual reading understands the page Output schema fixed fields in JSON honest null unreadable doesn’t mean guess

What it is:

What it is: A real-time voice system is a continuous loop of listening, deciding when a turn ends, thinking, and speaking—with a time budget for each step and the possibility of being interrupted at any moment.

Why learn it:

Voice support, triage, and scheduling are obvious use cases for Brazilian companies, which rely on phone calls and WhatsApp audio. Choosing the wrong architecture creates a bot that interrupts the customer or takes too long to respond—and that kills adoption faster than…

Key concepts:

STT / TTS speech ↔ text Cascade modular and auditable VAD detects when speech ends Barge-in allow interruptions

What it is:

What it is: Understanding video means turning it into sampled frames plus a transcript, then asking the model for a timeline of events. Generating video means synthesizing frames that are coherent with one another from text or an image, with cost and duration still limited.

Why learn it:

Video is the most expensive modality. Choosing the sampling rate based on measurement rather than guesswork can turn an unworkable project into a workable one—and knowing the limits of generation keeps you from promising leadership a whole AI-made commercial without review.

Key concepts:

Sampling fewer frames, lower cost Timeline events with timestamps Diffusion refined noise into an image Temporal consistency the same from start to finish

What it is:

What it is: A perception-and-action loop: capture the screen, decide, act, verify the result. A safe architecture puts guardrails around this loop with isolation, a list of permitted actions, a step limit, and a human approval gate for actions that can’t be undone.

Why learn it:

Computer use unlocks automation in places where only fragile RPA or manual data entry used to work. But it has the largest attack surface of any multimodal capability. A leader who knows to require a sandbox, least privilege, and a human approval gate can approve the pilot without exposing the company.

Key concepts:

Computer use agent operates the screen Sandbox isolated environment Indirect injection hidden instruction on the screen Human approval gate approval for irreversible actions

What it is:

What it is: Evaluation means comparing the system’s output with human-provided ground truth on the same dataset every time, using a metric for each field or word. Every change to the model, prompt, or resolution is tested again against this dataset before going to production.

Why learn it:

Without ground truth, choosing a model becomes a matter of opinion, and changing versions becomes a gamble. With it, you can compare providers on your own data, justify costs with your own numbers, and catch regressions before customers do.

Key concepts:

Ground truth correct human-provided answer Accuracy by field an average can hide problems WER / CER word and character error Regression something got worse after a change

What it is:

What it is: A multimodal product architecture is the chain preprocessing → routing → model → validation → human review, designed alongside the data protection rules for each type of data it handles.

Why learn it:

Multimodality multiplies value and risk at the same time: more types of data come in, costs go up, and more rules apply. Designing the full chain—cost, routing, evaluation, and LGPD—lets you deliver a product that passes legal and finance review, not just a…

Key concepts:

Preprocessing where costs go down Routing right model for each case Biometric data sensitive under LGPD Minimal retention store only what was extracted

🔬 AI Research and Papers

How to read a paper, reproduce results, question benchmarks, and turn research into a product.

What it is:

What it is: The path an idea takes from the lab to your product: preprint, review, open source, adoption by libraries, and finally a market feature. Each step trades speed for confidence.

Why learn it:

Architects and technical leaders make costly decisions—switching models, adopting a retrieval technique, building a fine-tuning team—based on what they read. Knowing which channel the information came from changes the size of the bet: a preprint justifies a test of…

Key concepts:

Preprint published before review Peer review filter, not a guarantee System card profile of a released model Speed × confidence the tradeoff at each channel

What it is:

What it is: A reading protocol with increasing depth: overview, understanding, and reconstruction. Each level ends with an explicit decision to stop or continue.

Why learn it:

The bottleneck for leaders isn’t access to papers; it’s attention. A protocol with explicit gates turns an infinite stack of reading into a short queue of decisions and lets the rest of the team know how deeply each paper was read.

Key concepts:

First pass Is it relevant? Second pass Understand the evidence Third pass Reconstruct and question Gate Stopping is also a decision

What it is:

What it is: A map of a paper’s sections and the question each one answers. Knowing the map lets you go straight to the passage that matters for your decision.

Why learn it:

Knowing where each answer is found turns hours of reading into a few focused minutes. For a manager, that means being able to ask the team the right question—“What was the ablation?”—instead of accepting the summary as proof.

Key concepts:

Ablation remove one component and measure the loss Baseline the comparison reference Error bar variation across runs Appendix where to find the reproduction

What it is:

What it is: A critical reading of evaluation numbers: understanding what the benchmark measures, whether it still distinguishes between models, and whether the result holds up on data the model has never seen—especially yours.

Why learn it:

Model launches come with benchmark tables selected by the people launching them. Readers who approach these numbers skeptically avoid unnecessary vendor switches and ask the team for the only evidence that settles the question: performance measured on the company’s data.

Key concepts:

Contamination test data leaked into training Saturation everyone scores at the ceiling Goodhart’s law the metric stops measuring what it was meant to Evaluation of your own data your cases, outside the internet

What it is:

What it is: The work of verifying a research claim with version control, seeds, recorded configuration, and multiple runs—first under the paper’s conditions, then under yours.

Why learn it:

Many decisions that “the new model is better” are made after a single run on a handful of examples. Requiring an average and variation is a low-cost rule that prevents expensive migrations based on luck—and gives the team a shared language for discussing results.

Key concepts:

Reproduce same method, same data Replicate works on your data Seed controls randomness Standard deviation how much the result varies

What it is:

What it is: A step-by-step adoption process—reading, reproduction, proof on your own data, pilot, and production—each with a pass criterion agreed on before starting.

Why learn it:

Research without an adoption process becomes one of two things: curiosity that never delivers, or a big bet without evidence. The gated ladder gives leadership a way to say “yes, with limits”—the balance that makes innovation possible without putting the product at risk.

Key concepts:

Adoption ladder one step at a time Predefined criterion written before the test Smallest proof the cheapest test that settles the question Rollback plan a safe way to go back

What it is:

What it is: A team routine with defined sources, standardized briefs, and a radar that tracks techniques in four states, reviewed at a fixed cadence.

Why learn it:

The value of tracking research shows up months later, when a problem arises and the team already knows which techniques were tested, which failed, and why. Without a record, everyone has to relearn things on their own, and knowledge walks out the door when someone changes jobs.

Key concepts:

Radar techniques by status Reading group one paper per week Decision brief traceable decision Owner name and due date

🎮 AI Reinforcement Learning

Agent, environment, and reward; RLHF, RL with verifiable rewards, and why RL made models reason.

What it is:

What it is: RL is learning a policy through trial and error, guided by rewards. The question it answers isn’t “What’s the right answer?” but “What sequence of decisions leads to the best cumulative result?”

Why learn it:

RL is no longer just a topic in robotics and games: it’s part of how all major assistants today are trained. Understanding the agent–environment–reward cycle helps you read model announcements, talk with vendors about fine-tuning, and anticipate behaviors…

Key concepts:

Agent who decides Reward the score for each step Policy state → action Return discounted sum of rewards

What it is:

What it is The technical core of RL: balancing exploration and exploitation, and choosing whether to learn the value of actions, learn the policy directly, or combine both approaches.

Why learn it:

The same concepts — value, policy, exploration, discounting — appear in language model training, recommendation systems, and agents. Watching an agent learn on a 4x4 grid gives you an intuition no diagram can: learning depends on how much it…

Key concepts:

Explore × exploit new or known Q-function value of each action Policy gradient adjusts the policy directly PPO stable actor-critic

What it is:

What it is Reward hacking happens when the reward increases but the real goal doesn’t improve — or gets worse. It happens because the reward is always an approximation (proxy) of what you want.

Why learn it:

Strange assistant behaviors — flattery, verbosity, “fixed” tests — make sense when you read them as reward hacking. If you plan to fine-tune models or evaluate agents, designing the reward and an independent metric is the part of the project…

Key concepts:

Proxy approximate measure Reward hacking shortcut that fools the score Flattery agreeing to please Independent metric what the agent can’t see

What it is:

What it is RLHF fine-tunes a language model with RL, using as its reward a model trained on human comparisons of responses, with a constraint that keeps it close to its original behavior.

Why learn it:

RLHF explains assistants’ “personality”: their tone, refusals, and tendency to agree. If you plan to fine-tune an open model with your own data, understanding the three steps and the variants helps you choose the least expensive option that works — often DPO with…

Key concepts:

Reward model predicts human preference KL penalty prevents drifting from the original RLAIF feedback from another model DPO preference without explicit RL

What it is:

What it is RLVR trains through reinforcement using a result checked by a program as the reward — a correct answer, passing tests, or a valid format — generating several attempts per problem and reinforcing the ones that perform best.

Why learn it:

Verifiable rewards are why models have improved so much at math and programming, where answer keys exist. For a company, the practical lesson is: if your task has a reliable verifier (tests, checkable business rules…

Key concepts:

Verifier checks; it doesn’t judge RLVR reward from a program GRPO compares within the group Hidden tests to prevent cheating

What it is:

What it is This is the shift of 2024–2025: applying RL with verifiable rewards to language models led to long, useful chains of reasoning and turned “thinking time” into a way to control quality and cost.

Why learn it:

Choosing between a fast model and a reasoning model is now one of the architecture decisions with the greatest impact on cost and quality. Understanding where reasoning comes from — RL with a verifier — helps you predict where it helps (tasks with steps and answer keys) and where it…

Key concepts:

Reasoning model thinks before answering Test-time compute spending more on the response Emergent behavior wasn’t taught by example Budget quality × cost

What it is:

What it is A decision criterion: use RL only when there are sequential decisions or a reliable verifier, simpler alternatives (prompting, RAG, SFT, DPO) have already been measured and aren’t enough, and there’s a budget for the environment and evaluation.

Why learn it:

RL is at the center of AI news, creating pressure to “use RL.” A leader who knows the conditions under which it works protects the team’s budget, chooses the least expensive alternative when it’s enough, and recognizes the rare case where an environment with…

Key concepts:

Use × train most people only use models Simulator safe place to make mistakes Alternatives ladder prompt → RAG → SFT → DPO → RL Hidden cost environment and evaluation

🛡️ Security and Red Teaming

Prompt injection, data leaks, agents with excessive permissions, red teaming, and layered defenses.

What it is:

What it is The set of points through which an attacker can influence an AI system: what the user types, the content the system reads (pages, files, emails, tool results), the tools it can trigger, and the channels through which…

Why learn it:

AI security is determined by architecture, not by the prompt. If you can see the attack surface before building, you can choose workflows that don’t combine all three capabilities — and avoid relying on a defense that no provider can fully guarantee today.

Key concepts:

Attack surface — where risk enters | OWASP LLM Top 10 — list of common risks | MITRE ATLAS — catalog of techniques | Lethal trifecta — data + untrusted input + output

What it is:

What it is: When user input tries to override or bypass the system instructions. You can’t eliminate it with a “stronger” prompt; you can only contain it by limiting what the system can do when it’s fooled.

Why learn it:

Every product with a text field open to the public gets injection attempts — out of curiosity, for fun, or with malicious intent. The difference between an embarrassing incident and a serious one is whether the system was designed on the assumption that the model can be…

Key concepts:

Direct injection — the user attacks | Jailbreak — bypass the provider’s policy | System prompt — assume it will leak | Worst case — the attacker controls the output

What it is:

What it is: Injection that arrives through third-party data the system consumes. The attacker doesn’t need access to your application — they just need to put text somewhere it will read.

Why learn it:

Indirect injection is the risk growing fastest as agents are adopted, because it turns any internet content into a potential attack vector. Leaders who approve integrations — “let’s have the agent read emails and reply” — need to ask the question…

Key concepts:

Indirect injection — the attack comes through content | Untrusted content — everything that comes from outside | Poisoned RAG — hostile document in the knowledge base | Tool poisoning — instructions in the tool description

What it is:

What it is: Excessive agency means giving an agent more tools, access, or autonomy than the task requires. Since the agent can be fooled, an attacker can do anything the agent can do.

Why learn it:

Since prompt injection has no complete solution, the agent’s permissions set the ceiling on the damage. Reducing functionality, access, and autonomy is the most reliable defense available today — and the cheapest to apply before putting the agent into production.

Key concepts:

Excessive agency — functionality, access, autonomy | Least privilege — only what’s needed | Human approval — for irreversible actions | Sandbox — isolated execution

What it is:

What it is: Taking data from inside to outside through something the system produces: an image, a link, a tool call, a message. The defense is to close or filter every output channel, not just protect the input.

Why learn it:

Injection is the means; exfiltration is the damage. Closing output channels is the layer that turns a successful injection attack into an incident without a data leak — and, when personal data is involved, it’s the difference between an internal record and a mandatory notification to…

Key concepts:

Exfiltration — data leaves without authorization | Image — Markdown leaks data when rendered | Egress — control of network output | LGPD incident — notify the ANPD and data subjects

What it is:

What it is: Stacking independent controls — input, isolation, permissions, output, network, and monitoring — so a failure in one doesn’t compromise the system. Controls enforced in code are worth more than controls in the prompt.

Why learn it:

Model providers improve resistance to injection with every release, but none promises immunity. A layered architecture takes system security out of the hands of a single component you don’t control and lets you switch models without repeating the analysis of…

Key concepts:

Defense in depth — multiple independent layers | Spotlighting — mark what is data | Reader × executor — the reader doesn’t act | Canary — fake secret that reveals a leak

What it is:

What it is: An authorized, documented exercise in attacking your own system: scope, test cases, repeated execution, severity classification, fixes, and retesting — with the test suite becoming part of the release pipeline.

Why learn it:

Even a small internal red team finds the obvious flaws before the first curious user does. And an automated test suite turns security from a one-time event into a habit: every model change faces the same attacks, and leadership gets a number…

Key concepts:

Red teaming — attack your own system | Authorization — scope and timeline in writing | Break rate — repeat each case | Regression — retest after every change

🏢 AI in Production — Enterprise Scale

Internal AI platform, gateway, identity, auditing, SLAs, FinOps, and adoption across the company.

What it is:

What it is: An internal AI platform is the shared layer between applications and models: a gateway through which all calls pass, shared services (context, evaluation, tools), and three cross-cutting controls — identity, auditing, and cost.

Why learn it:

You pay for a platform once; without one, you pay the cost with every new project, through rework, incidents, and audits. For architects, the question is no longer “which model should we use?” but “how can any team use any model securely and without…”

Key concepts:

Internal product platform whose customers are the teams Paved path: the right way is the easiest way Shared service: built once, used by everyone Cross-cutting controls: identity, auditing, cost

What it is:

What it is: A single entry point for all the company’s models. It separates the logical name the app requests from the physical model that responds, and applies authentication, limits, caching, fallback, and logging to every call.

Why learn it:

Models change every few months, and so do prices. Without a gateway, every switch becomes a migration project across ten apps. With a gateway, it’s one tested configuration line in one place—and that same entry point becomes the foundation for auditing, limits, and cost, which come…

Key concepts:

Logical route: name the capability, not the provider Fallback: automatic Plan B Team limits: calls and tokens per minute Compatible API: one format, many models

What it is:

What it is: The set of rules that ties each AI call to an identified person or agent, applies that identity’s role to the data and tools accessed, and maintains the minimum privileges needed for the task.

Why learn it:

The most likely leaks in enterprise AI aren’t sophisticated attacks on the model; they’re inherited permissions that weren’t set up correctly. A prompt can be bypassed; a permission filter applied before the search can’t. Getting identity right from the start prevents you from having to rebuild…

Key concepts:

SSO: one corporate account RBAC: role-based access Service identity: the agent has its own account Least privilege: only what’s needed

What it is:

What it is: Traceability is the ability to reconstruct any response: who asked, with which configuration, what data went in, what the model and tools did, and how much it cost—while keeping only what’s needed for only as long as needed.

Why learn it:

Without traceability, every incident becomes one person’s word against another’s, and the only safe response is to shut the system down. With it, you can find the cause, fix the prompt version or the incorrect document, and show legal and the customer exactly what happened.

Key concepts:

Trace: follow the request end to end Span: a step with timing and attributes Prompt version: a prompt is versioned code Retention: how long to keep it and why

What it is:

What it is: Set measurable targets for availability, latency, and quality for each use case, measure continuously, and change versions (model, prompt, index) only in stages with gates that check those targets.

Why learn it:

Promising an SLA without measuring quality means promising something you don’t control. Teams that set SLOs across all three areas and make changes through gates can update models often without fear—and teams that don’t end up freezing the version out of concern and falling behind.

Key concepts:

SLI · SLO · SLA: measure, target, contract p95: tail latency, not the average Canary: new version for a small portion of traffic Rollback: a tested way to revert

What it is:

What it is: AI FinOps means measuring the cost of each call, assigning it to a team and use case, showing it to decision-makers (showback) or charging for it internally (chargeback), and optimizing for cost per outcome—not cost per token.

Why learn it:

The first AI budget cut almost never comes from a use case that failed; it comes from a bill nobody could explain. When you assign costs and measure them by outcome, you can defend the use cases that pay for themselves and end the ones that don’t, with the numbers in hand.

Key concepts:

Showback: show the cost to the people using it Chargeback: charge internally Cost per outcome: the metric that matters Per-agent cap: limit steps and spending

What it is:

What it is: The work of bringing the platform into real use: providing secure access, training people by role, maintaining a catalog of use cases, and measuring usage, results, and satisfaction—reducing shadow AI by offering a better option, not by banning it.

What it is:

What it is AGI is the term for general-purpose AI with human-level or higher performance across a wide range of tasks. There’s no test everyone accepts that tells us “it’s here”; so the useful question is “which definition, measured how?”.

Why learn it:

Leaders are asked to weigh in on AGI at board meetings, events, and in the press. Knowing that the term has several definitions lets you turn a vague conversation into a verifiable question—and avoid both promising what no one knows and dismissing what already…

Key concepts:

AGI general AI, definition disputed Generality how many different tasks Autonomy how much it acts without supervision Narrow AI excellent at one task

What it is:

What it is It’s a way to read: separate every claim about AI’s future into what’s known, what’s debated, and what’s speculation, then make decisions based on the first two boxes, treating the third as a possible scenario, not a fact.

Why learn it:

You’ll read far more predictions than measurements. Separating the three boxes protects the organization from two kinds of waste: investing out of fear of speculation and ignoring what has already been measured and is changing your sector.

Key concepts:

Scaling laws more data and computing, better model Uneven frontier excellent here, weak there Primary source who measured, not who commented Three boxes known, debated, speculation

What it is:

Why learn it:

Key concepts:
