🗺️ The research ecosystem

AI research moves faster than the traditional scientific publishing system. Almost every relevant work first appears on arXiv, an open repository of preprints (versions published before any peer review). Months later, some of these works go through a conference review—NeurIPS, ICML, and ICLR for machine learning in general; ACL and EMNLP for language; CVPR for vision. In parallel, labs publish technical reports e system cards (documents describing a released model, its evaluations, and its risks) that never undergo external review.

For decision-makers, the takeaway is simple: the channel tells you how much to trust it, not whether it is true. A preprint may be excellent; a conference paper may not hold up in your domain; a lab’s technical report contains valuable data, but it is written by the people selling the model. You read them all, with varying degrees of caution.

What it is

This is the path an idea takes from the lab to your product: preprint, review, open-source code, adoption by libraries, and finally a market feature. Each stage trades speed for trust.

FontWhat it offersCaution
arXiv (cs.CL, cs.LG, cs.AI)Almost everything, first.No review; versions change (v1, v2…).
ConferencesReviewed papers and topic-focused workshops.Review filters out major errors; it does not guarantee the results can be replicated.

Why learn

Architects and technical leaders make costly decisions—switching models, adopting a retrieval technique, building a fine-tuning team—based on what they read. Knowing which channel the information came from changes the size of the bet: a preprint justifies a one-week test, not a system rewrite.

📖 Three-pass reading

No one reads every cutting-edge paper from beginning to end. The most cited method for not getting lost is the three-pass method, described by S. Keshav in “How to Read a Paper” (2007). The idea: each pass is a decision. After the first, you decide whether a second is worthwhile; after the second, whether a third is worthwhile. Most papers stop at the first—and that’s success, not laziness.

In the first pass you read the title, abstract, introduction, section headings, and conclusions, and look at the figures. In the second one, you read the text carefully, understand the figures and tables, and note references you don’t know, without getting caught up in the proofs. In the third, you try to reconstruct the work mentally—or in code—and question every assumption. Only papers that could change one of your decisions deserve a third pass.

What it is

A protocol for reading with increasing depth: overview, understanding, and reconstruction. Each level ends with an explicit decision to stop or continue.

🔎 Terms

Contribution is what the paper claims to add that is new (method, data, result, analysis). Implicit assumption is a condition the authors assume without stating—for example, that the test data resemble the production data. Related work is the section that positions the paper in relation to others; that’s where you discover the idea’s competitors.

Goal: do the first and second passes with an assistant, without outsourcing your judgment.

You are a skeptical technical reviewer. I’ve attached the PDF of the paper <paper title>.
Our context: <your team’s product, data, and constraints, in 3 lines>

Respond in English, in this order, citing the section or page for each claim:
1. Contribution: what the paper claims is new, in no more than 3 sentences.
2. Evidence: which experiments support each claim (table/figure).
3. Baselines: what do they compare against? Are the baselines recent and well tuned?
4. Implicit assumptions: what must be true for the result to hold?
5. Limitations: those the authors acknowledge and those you suspect.
6. Reproducibility: have the code, weights, data, and hyperparameters been published?
7. For us: what would change in our context if the result is confirmed?
If the PDF doesn’t provide a piece of information, write “not reported”—don’t infer it.
End with: STOP / SECOND PASS / THIRD PASS and the reason.

How to check: Choose two claims from the response and open the cited section or table. If the citation doesn’t check out, the summary isn’t useful—try again, asking for exact excerpts. Compare item 3 with what you know about the state of the art; that’s where the assistant most often makes mistakes by omission.

Why learn

The bottleneck for leaders isn’t access to papers; it’s attention. A protocol with explicit gates turns an infinite stack of reading into a short queue of decisions and lets the rest of the team know how deeply each paper was read.

🧩 Anatomy of a paper

AI papers follow an almost fixed structure, and each part answers a different question. The abstract sells; the introduction lists the contributions; the method says what was done; the experiments show numbers; the ablation (an experiment that removes one component at a time to measure how much each contributes) shows what really matters; and the appendix holds the details that determine whether you can reproduce the work.

A good example of productive reading is “Attention Is All You Need” itself (Vaswani and colleagues, 2017), which introduced the Transformer. The title talks about attention, but readers who went to the table of architecture variations saw which choices mattered most to the result—information that, years later, guided many engineering decisions. The lesson applies to any paper: the argument is in the text, but the evidence is in the tables.

What it is

This is a map of a paper’s sections and the question each one answers. Knowing the map lets you go straight to the passage that matters for your decision.

SectionQuestion it answersWhat to look for
SummaryWhy should I read this?The central claim, in one sentence.
IntroductionWhat’s new?The list of contributions (usually numbered).
Related workWho are the competitors?Competitors left out of the experiments.
MethodWhat exactly was done?Architecture diagram, loss function, data.
ExperimentsDoes it work?Baselines, number of runs, error bars.
AblationWhich component matters?Does the gain disappear when the main idea is removed?
LimitationsWhere does it fail?Untested domains, languages, and scales.
AppendixCan you reproduce it?Hyperparameters, prompts, computing costs.

💡 Tip

In language model papers, look in the appendix for the exact prompts used in the evaluation. Small changes in prompt format can change results; if the paper doesn’t publish the prompts, treat the numbers as approximate.

✓ Signs of a solid paper

  • ✓Ablation shows where the gain comes from.
  • ✓Multiple runs, with variation reported.
  • ✓Recent baselines tuned with the same care.
  • ✓A specific limitations section, not a generic one.

✗ Warning signs

  • ✗A small gain without error bars or based on only one run.
  • ✗Comparison with older versions of the competitor.
  • ✗“State of the art” on a single chosen benchmark.
  • ✗Missing training details or prompts.

Why learn

Knowing where each answer is found turns hours of reading into a few focused minutes. For a manager, that means being able to ask the team the right question—“What was the ablation?”—instead of accepting the summary as proof.

📊 Why benchmarks can mislead

A A benchmark is a fixed set of tasks with known answers, used to compare models — MMLU for general knowledge, HumanEval for code, SWE-bench for fixing real repository issues, GPQA for difficult science questions. They are useful and necessary. The problem is that as soon as a number becomes a public target, it starts losing its ability to measure what it promised. This is Goodhart’s law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.

Three mechanisms make this happen. Contamination: test questions appear, intentionally or not, in the training data, and the model “remembers” instead of solving them. Saturation: the best models approach the ceiling, and the remaining differences become noise or answer key errors. Test-set tuning: prompt, sampling, and configuration choices are made by looking at the benchmark itself, inflating the result. Human preference leaderboards, such as LMArena, measure something else — what people prefer in a conversation — and can also be specifically optimized for.

What it is

It means critically reading evaluation numbers: understanding what the benchmark measures, whether it still distinguishes between models, and whether the result holds up on data the model has never seen — especially your own.

✓ Questions that protect you

  • ✓Does the benchmark resemble my real task?
  • ✓Is there a new or “clean” version of the test, published after the model’s cutoff date?
  • ✓Were competitors evaluated with the same prompt and configuration?
  • ✓How much variation is there across runs?

✗ Common pitfalls

  • ✗Choosing a model based on a one-point difference on a leaderboard.
  • ✗Comparing numbers from papers that used different configurations.
  • ✗Treating an English benchmark as proof of performance in Portuguese.
  • ✗Ignoring cost and latency alongside quality.

💡 Tip

Build your own evaluation set — a few dozen to a few hundred real cases, anonymized in accordance with the LGPD (Brazil’s data protection law), with expected answers. It never goes on the internet, so it doesn’t contaminate training data, and it measures exactly what matters for your product. It’s the most valuable research asset an applied team can have.

Why learn

Model launches come with benchmark tables selected by the people launching them. Readers who approach these numbers skeptically avoid unnecessary vendor switches and ask the team for the only evidence that settles the question: performance measured on the company’s data.

🔁 Reproducing results

Reproducing means getting the same result with the same method and data; replicating means getting a compatible result with different data or conditions — in your case, your company’s data. Both fail more often than you might think, almost always for the same reasons: an unpublished hyperparameter, a different prompt, a library version, or a single lucky run.

There’s an added complication with language model systems: the output is stochastic (it has randomness controlled by temperature and the seed). A result measured once is a sample, not a fact. Before trusting an improvement, run it several times with different seeds and look at the average and variation. If the improvement is smaller than the variation across runs, you still don’t know whether it exists.

What it is

It means confirming a research claim by tracking versions, seeds, and recorded settings, and running the experiment multiple times — first under the conditions in the paper, then with your own data.

Step 1 · Freeze the environment. Record library, model, and prompt versions in a file alongside the code.

Step 2 · Reproduce the paper’s number. Use the same benchmark and configuration. If the result doesn’t match, investigate before moving on.

Step 3 · Measure the variation. Run five or more times with different seeds; report the mean and standard deviation.

Step 4 · Replicate with your data. Compare it with your current baseline on your evaluation set.

Step 5 · Record the verdict. It reproduced, partially reproduced, or did not reproduce — and why.

Try this: measure whether a result repeats across seeds, using a local model in Ollama (Python 3 standard library only).

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""repro.py — measures whether a result repeats across seeds.
Usage:   python3 repro.py cases.jsonl --modelo llama3.2 --seeds 1 2 3 4 5
Each JSONL line: {"pergunta": "...", "resposta": "..."}  (short answers)
Requires: ollama pull llama3.2  (server at http://localhost:11434)
"""
import argparse, json, statistics, urllib.request

# OpenAI-compatible paid API: change the URL and add the Authorization header.
URL = "http://localhost:11434/v1/chat/completions"

def perguntar(modelo, texto, seed, temperatura):
    corpo = json.dumps({
        "model": modelo,
        "messages": [{"role": "user",
                      "content": texto + "\nResponda só com a resposta final, sem explicação."}],
        "temperature": temperatura,
        "seed": seed,
    }).encode()
    req = urllib.request.Request(URL, data=corpo, headers={"Content-Type": "application/json"})
    with urllib.request.urlopen(req, timeout=180) as r:
        return json.load(r)["choices"][0]["message"]["content"].strip()

def normalizar(s):
    return " ".join(s.lower().strip(" .!\n").split())

def main():
    ap = argparse.ArgumentParser()
    ap.add_argument("arquivo")
    ap.add_argument("--modelo", default="llama3.2")
    ap.add_argument("--seeds", type=int, nargs="+", default=[1, 2, 3, 4, 5])
    ap.add_argument("--temperatura", type=float, default=0.7)
    a = ap.parse_args()
    casos = [json.loads(l) for l in open(a.arquivo, encoding="utf-8") if l.strip()]
    taxas = []
    for seed in a.seeds:
        ok = sum(normalizar(perguntar(a.modelo, c["pergunta"], seed, a.temperatura))
                 == normalizar(c["resposta"]) for c in casos)
        taxas.append(ok / len(casos))
        print(f"seed {seed}: {ok}/{len(casos)} = {ok / len(casos):.1%}")
    media = statistics.mean(taxas)
    desvio = statistics.stdev(taxas) if len(taxas) > 1 else 0.0
    print(f"\nmodelo={a.modelo} temperatura={a.temperatura} casos={len(casos)} seeds={len(a.seeds)}")
    print(f"acurácia: {media:.1%} ± {desvio:.1%} (desvio-padrão entre seeds)")

if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

How to check: create a cases.jsonl with 20 to 50 short-answer questions (number, yes/no, one word) and run it twice with the same seeds: the numbers should be identical or very close. Then run it with --temperatura 0 and compare the deviation. When testing a prompt or model change, accept the improvement only if the difference between the averages is greater than the deviation.

Why learn

Many decisions that “the new model is better” are made after a single run on a handful of examples. Requiring an average and variation is a low-cost rule that prevents expensive migrations based on luck—and gives the team a shared language for discussing results.

🏭 From paper to product

Some papers became infrastructure for the entire field. “Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks” (Lewis et al., 2020) gave RAG its name. “LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models” (Hu et al., 2021) made fine-tuning inexpensive. “Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models” (Wei et al., 2022) changed how people write prompts, and “ReAct” (Yao et al., 2022) established the pattern of reasoning and acting with tools that underlies agents. In every case, the path to a product involved many teams testing the idea under real-world conditions.

For a company, the question isn’t “Is this paper good?” but “What’s the smallest piece of evidence that justifies the next investment?” A ladder with explicit gates prevents two opposite mistakes: ignoring a technique that has already become standard and rewriting the system because of last week’s preprint.

What it is

It’s a step-by-step adoption process — reading, reproduction, validation on your own data, a pilot, and production — with a pass criterion agreed on in advance for each step.

StepPass criterion (example)Decision-maker
Read and screenAddresses a problem on our roadmap.Technical lead
ReproducingPaper’s result reproduced within the reported variation.Engineering
ValidateImprovement greater than the deviation on your own evaluation set.Engineering + product
PilotBusiness metric improves, cost per task stays within the cap.Product + management
ProductionMonitoring, rollback plan, and an assigned owner.Management

💡 Tip

Before implementing anything, check whether the technique is already available in a maintained library (Hugging Face Transformers, PEFT, TRL, LangGraph) or as a resource from your provider. Implementing a paper from scratch only makes sense when the advantage justifies maintaining the code indefinitely.

Why learn

Research without an adoption process becomes one of two things: curiosity that never delivers, or a big bet without evidence. The gated ladder gives leadership a way to say “yes, with limits”—the balance that makes innovation possible without putting the product at risk.

Adoption ladder

Prior criterion

Smallest proof

📡 The team’s research radar

Keeping up with research isn’t a heroic individual task; it’s a team routine. A research radar is a living list of techniques classified by status — watch, test, adopt, discard — kept up to date with short briefs and reviewed at a regular meeting. It works because it divides the reading, records the reasoning, and turns “I saw a cool paper” into a traceable decision.

The simplest format is a journal club : each week, one person presents a paper in fifteen minutes using the brief from topic 2, and the group decides which ring of the radar it belongs in. AI assistants speed up triage, but a named person makes the decision and completes the final brief.

What it is

A team routine with defined sources, standardized briefs, and a radar of techniques in four statuses, reviewed on a fixed schedule.

👀 Watch

Promising, but not yet proven outside the paper. Revisit in three months.

🧪 Test

Has an owner and a deadline to reproduce and validate it on your own data.

✅ Adopt

Passed the gates; becomes the team standard.

🗄️ Discard

Couldn’t be reproduced or doesn’t fit the context. Record why.

Goal: standard entry for the radar (copy it into a wiki, repository, or spreadsheet).

# radar/<short-technique-name>.yaml
technique: <technique name, e.g., reranking with a smaller model>
paper: "<exact title>"
authors_year: "<first author and colleagues, year>"
link: <arXiv or conference URL>
channel: preprint | conference | technical-report
read_through: pass-1 | pass-2 | pass-3
owner: <person's name>
brief_date: <YYYY-MM-DD>

central_claim: <one sentence>
evidence: <table/figure and what it shows>
code_published: yes | no | partial
our_problem: <which roadmap problem this addresses>

status: watch | test | adopt | discard
next_gate:
  criterion: <e.g., improvement greater than the variation on your own evaluation set>
  deadline: <YYYY-MM-DD>
reproduction_result: pending | reproduced | partial | not-reproduced
notes: <what was surprising, risks, estimated cost>
review_on: <YYYY-MM-DD>

How to check: an entry is ready when another person on the team can say what the technique promises, what its status is, and what the next test is without reading the paper. Every entry in “test” needs an owner and a deadline; without those, move it back to “watch.” If you save it as YAML, python3 -c "import yaml,sys; yaml.safe_load(open(sys.argv[1]))" radar/<arquivo>.yaml confirms that the format is valid (requires pip install pyyaml).

Try this: use an assistant to review the radar monthly based on the briefs.

Below are the briefs from our research radar (YAML format).
<paste the briefs here>

Conduct the monthly review:
1. List entries in “test” with overdue deadlines or no owner.
2. List entries in “watch” whose review_on date has passed.
3. Point out techniques that seem to address the same problem and could be tested together.
4. For each entry in “adopt,” ask what needs to be in place in production
   (monitoring, rollback plan, owner) and mark what’s missing.
Don’t invent papers, results, or dates: use only what’s in the briefs.
Format: one table per item, then 3 suggested decisions for the meeting.

How to check: Manually check two rows in the table against the original briefs. If a paper or date appears that isn’t in the briefs, discard the response and repeat, reinforcing the last instruction.

Why learn

The value of tracking research shows up months later, when a problem arises and the team already knows which techniques were tested, which failed, and why. Without a record, everyone has to relearn things on their own, and knowledge walks out the door when someone changes jobs.
