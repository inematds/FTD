🌌 The Future of AI and AGI

Two loud narratives circulate about the future of AI: that everything will change next year and that everything will go wrong. This module takes the middle path, using a method: separate what’s known, what’s debated, and what’s speculation, look at the trends already emerging, address risks with both feet on the ground, and turn uncertainty into a plan.

🌌 What AGI means

AGI (Artificial General Intelligence, is a term with no single definition, and much of the heated debate comes from people using different definitions without realizing it. The common idea is a system that isn’t good at just one task, but at a wide variety of cognitive tasks, at a level comparable to that of skilled people.

Three families of definitions come up often. The economic: OpenAI’s 2018 charter describes highly autonomous systems that outperform humans at most economically valuable work. The level-based: a 2023 paper by Google DeepMind researchers proposes measuring performance and generality separately, and treating autonomy as another dimension. And the cognitive: abilities such as continuously learning new things, planning for the long term, and transferring knowledge to unfamiliar situations. These definitions don’t lead to the same place or point to the same date.

What it is

AGI is the name given to general-purpose AI that performs at a human or higher level across a broad range of tasks. There’s no test accepted by everyone that says “it’s here”; so the useful question is “which definition, measured how?”

✓ Questions that clarify

  • ✓“Which definition of AGI are you using?”
  • ✓“Measured how, on which tasks, compared with whom?”
  • ✓“With how much autonomy, and for how long at a time?”

✗ Common misunderstandings

  • ✗Treating AGI as an event on a specific day, rather than as capabilities that arrive gradually and unevenly.
  • ✗Confusing fluent conversation with understanding or having intentions.
  • ✗Using a single benchmark as proof of general intelligence.

Why learn

Leaders are asked to weigh in on AGI at board meetings, events, and in the press. If you know the term has several definitions, you can turn a vague conversation into a question you can verify—and avoid both promising what no one knows and dismissing what is already happening.

🔎 What we know and what is speculation

The most useful tool in this module is simple: for each claim about AI’s future, ask which of three categories it belongs in. Known: there are published measurements repeated by different groups. Debated: qualified experts looking at the same data disagree. Speculation: a prediction or opinion, sometimes from highly knowledgeable people, but without measurements to support it.

Some examples of what belongs in the first category: increasing data, parameters, and compute improved models predictably for several years (the so-called scaling laws, described in papers from 2020 and 2022); reinforcement training with verifiable rewards helped models improve at math and coding; and performance is uneven —the “jagged frontier,” a term from a 2023 study with consultants that showed gains on some tasks and poorer results on other apparently similar ones.

What it is

This is a way to read: separate each claim about AI’s future into known, debated, or speculation, then make decisions based on the first two categories, treating the third as a possible scenario rather than a fact.

Try this: run an AI news story or prediction through the three categories.

I’m going to paste a text (news story, post, talk, or report) about AI’s future.
Text: <paste here>

1. List each claim in the text separately, one per line.
2. Classify each one:
   KNOWN        = there are published measurements repeated by different groups
   DEBATED      = qualified experts disagree about the same data
   SPECULATION  = a prediction or opinion without measurements to support it
3. For each KNOWN claim, say what kind of primary source would confirm it
   (scientific paper, model card, independent evaluation report).
   Don’t invent titles, authors, or links.
4. Point out hype or fearmongering words and rewrite the claim neutrally.
5. For the 3 claims most relevant to <my sector>: what would change in my
   organization if they were true, and what action is worth taking even if they aren’t?

How to check: Choose two claims classified as KNOWN and find the primary source yourself. If you can’t find it, reclassify the claim—the model can also be overconfident. Repeat the exercise with an optimistic text and a pessimistic one: the proportion of speculation is usually similar in both.

Why learn

You’ll read far more predictions than measurements. Separating the three boxes protects the organization from two kinds of waste: investing out of fear of speculation and ignoring what has already been measured and is changing your sector.

📡 Trends already emerging

The safest way to think about the near future is to look at what is already happening on a small scale and ask what happens when it becomes cheap and reliable. As of October 2026, six trends are visible in products and public measurements—not in promises. They don’t tell us when AGI will arrive; they tell us what will change in your work over the next few years.

One indicator worth tracking is the task horizon: how long an agent can work on a task before making an unrecoverable mistake. Independent evaluation organizations such as METR publish measurements of this kind; they are more informative for a manager than a score on a question-and-answer benchmark.

What it is

These are changes already observable in products and public evaluations—agents working on long tasks, computer use, reasoning models, real-time multimodality, open and local models, and falling cost per capability—tracked through concrete signals, not announcements.

🤖 Agents on long tasks

Coding agents such as Claude Code and Codex already work for long periods in a repository. Signal: does the measured task horizon keep growing?

🖱️ Computer use

Models that operate screens, clicks, and forms. Signal: success rate in legacy systems without an API.

🧮 RL-trained reasoning

Models that “think before answering” in math, coding, and analysis. Signal: do the gains carry over to tasks without verifiable answers?

🎙️ Real-time multimodal

Voice, video, and documents in the same conversation. Signal: voice support resolving complete cases.

💻 Open and local models

Models that run on your own server through Ollama and similar tools. Signal: Can the local model handle your sensitive task?

📉 Cost per capability

The price for the same level of quality has fallen over the years. Signal: Have cases that were unfeasible last year become viable?

💡 Tip

For each trend, keep a case from your business that isn’t viable today but would become viable if the trend advanced. When the signal changes, you’ll know what to test first—and you won’t need to guess dates.

Why learn

Observable trends give an advantage to those who track signals instead of headlines. A short list of signals, reviewed regularly, lets you act early in the right cases without betting the company on a prediction.

⚠️ Risks without panic

AI risks range from very concrete to very uncertain. The concrete ones include misuse (fraud using fake voices and videos, automated cyberattacks, large-scale disinformation) and failures (the same error repeated across thousands of cases, agents with too many permissions). In between are structuralrisks: power concentrated among a few providers, technological dependence, unequal access. At the uncertain extreme is the debate about the alignment of systems far more capable than today’s.

Alignment is the problem of making a system actually pursue what you intend, rather than an approximation that works in tests and fails outside them. It’s a serious research area, with dedicated labs and academic researchers, and experts genuinely disagree about its severity and timeline. The mature approach is neither to ignore it nor to panic: know which layer each risk belongs to and what’s within your reach.

What it is

It’s a layered risk map—misuse, failures, structural risks, and long-term alignment—combined with a practical question: what does my organization control, what can it influence, and what can it only monitor?

✓ Useful approach

  • ✓Layered defenses and least privilege for agents (Module 3.6).
  • ✓Protocols against fraud using fake voices and videos: confirm payments and data through another channel.
  • ✓An exit plan for every critical vendor, including a tested open model.

✗ Approaches that get in the way

  • ✗Panic: ban everything and drive use into shadow AI.
  • ✗Denial: using “it’s just autocomplete” as an excuse to have no controls.
  • ✗Discussing only distant risks while leaving today’s concrete risks without an owner.

Why learn

Boards and teams will ask, “Is this dangerous?” A layered response—what’s concrete, what’s uncertain, and what we’re doing about it—builds trust and leads to action. Panic or dismissive responses lead to paralysis and incidents, respectively.

💼 The future of work

The idea from Module 1.1 still applies at the top of the organization: AI affects tasks, not entire professions. Exposure studies estimate how many tasks in each occupation AI can perform or speed up; exposure, however, doesn’t mean replacement. A highly exposed role can be augmented (one person does more and better) or reduced (fewer people do the same work)—and that depends on management decisions, not just the technology.

One concrete concern already appearing in discussions about HR and education is the entry-level step. Many tasks that used to train junior professionals—initial research, first drafts, simple code, document checks—are precisely the easiest to delegate to AI. If nobody does those tasks anymore, how will the senior professional of ten years from now get trained? Organizations thinking about this are designing new learning paths instead of simply cutting positions.

What it is

It’s the reorganization of work task by task: some tasks move to agents, others become more important (judgment, verification, relationships, agent coordination), and roles are redesigned—including how people just starting out are trained.

Task typeTrendWhat the leader does
Drafting, research, formattingIncreasingly delegatedRedesigns the workflow and measures quality, not volume.
Verification and reviewBecomes more importantTrains reviewers and defines what’s “good enough.”
Agent coordinationA new role in many areasCreates roles and training paths for orchestrating agents.
Relationships and decisionsRemains human and becomes more visibleFrees up time for it and holds people accountable for its results.
Tasks that train junior professionalsAt risk of disappearingMaintains deliberate learning paths with mentoring.

✓ Decisions that help people grow

  • ✓Reinvest some of the time saved in customer service, quality, and new products.
  • ✓Retrain people who know the process before hiring from outside.
  • ✓Communicate what is changing in advance and with clear criteria.

✗ Decisions that often come back to haunt you

  • ✗Cut before measuring whether quality holds up without those people.
  • ✗Eliminate all entry-level positions and discover years later that you’re short on senior talent.
  • ✗Treat “high exposure” as a sentence of replacement.

Why learn

AI’s effect on work in your organization isn’t set in stone: it depends on the decisions you and other leaders make about redesigning roles, training, and reinvestment. Understanding the difference between exposure and replacement is what lets you make those decisions with clear criteria.

🧭 Scenarios instead of predictions

No one knows exactly how fast AI will advance. In that kind of uncertainty, point predictions (“X will happen in 2029”) aren’t very useful for making decisions. Scenario planning, used for decades in energy and government, replaces the question “What will happen?” with “What would we do in each plausible future?”

The method selects two important, independent uncertainties, combines them, and describes the four resulting worlds. For AI, a useful pair is the pace of capability advances (slow or fast) and the speed of adoption in your industry (slow or fast). That leads to three types of action: no-regret moves (good in every scenario), bets (good in one or two, made when a signal appears), and safeguards (limit the damage in the worst-case scenario).

What it is

A planning method that combines two uncertainties to describe four plausible futures, defines signals that indicate which one is taking shape, and separates no-regret moves, conditional bets, and safeguards.

Goal: build the four scenarios for your organization in a work session.

Act as a neutral scenario-planning facilitator.
Organization: <industry, size, main products and customers>
Time horizon: <e.g., 3 years>
Selected uncertainties:
  Axis A: pace of capability advances in AI models (slow | fast)
  Axis B: speed of AI adoption in my industry (slow | fast)

1. Describe the 4 scenarios (A × B) in up to 5 lines each.
   No dates, no numbers, and no invented product names.
2. For each scenario, list 3 observable signals that would indicate we are
   entering it, and where I would track each signal.
3. List 5 NO-REGRET MOVES: good in all 4 scenarios.
4. For each scenario, list 2 BETS and the signal that would justify making them.
5. For the scenario least favorable to us, list 2 SAFEGUARDS.
6. List the assumptions you made about my organization.

How to check: Test each “no-regret” move in all four quadrants by asking, “In the gradual-progress scenario, is it still worth the cost?” If it isn’t in any of them, it’s a bet and belongs on a different list. Your team needs to be able to observe the signals; “AGI arrives” isn’t a signal, but “a competitor automates customer service from end to end” is.

Why learn

Scenarios help leaders escape the trap of waiting for certainty before acting. You act now on no-regret moves, prepare bets without spending on them, and know exactly which signal changes the plan—making the organization faster precisely when the future brings surprises.

🛠️ How to prepare methodically

Preparing for AI’s future doesn’t require you to guess. It takes three habits. A radar on a fixed schedule, fed by primary sources: papers, model cards and system cards from labs, independent evaluation reports (such as those from METR and Epoch AI), and Stanford University’s AI Index. An in-house test: a small set of your real tasks, run every quarter on the available models. And ongoing learning for the team, with practice on work-related tasks.

The in-house test is the most underestimated. Public benchmarks measure what labs chose to measure; your own set measures what matters to you. With it, the question “Is the new model better?” gets a five-minute answer backed by a history of results, instead of an impression. And when a task goes from “not feasible” to “feasible,” that change appears on your dashboard before it makes the headlines.

What it is

A preparation routine: a quarterly radar using primary sources, an in-house test of the organization’s tasks run with each new model, and ongoing team training—all recorded for comparison over time.

Every month · A short read. 30 minutes with primary sources; run each item you read through the three boxes in Topic 2.

Every quarter · Your own test. Run the script below on the models you use and the candidates; compare the results with the history.

Every quarter · Signals from the scenarios. Update the signals in Topic 6 and decide whether any bet should come off the shelf.

Every year · Strategy review. Portfolio, platform, team, and scenarios reviewed together, with decisions recorded.

Try this: your own test, running locally with Ollama and keeping a history.

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""bench_pessoal.py — your tasks, your criteria, measured every quarter.
Runs locally with Ollama (http://localhost:11434). Usage: python3 bench_pessoal.py llama3.2
For a paid model, change the URL and format to match the provider's API (or your gateway)."""
import json, sys, datetime, urllib.request

MODELO = sys.argv[1] if len(sys.argv) > 1 else "llama3.2"
# Replace these with YOUR real tasks, always anonymized (LGPD).
# "deve_conter" = terms a good answer needs to include.
TAREFAS = [
    {"id": "resumo-contrato",
     "prompt": "Summarize in 3 items: <anonymized contract excerpt>",
     "deve_conter": ["deadline", "penalty"]},
    {"id": "classificar-chamado",
     "prompt": "Classify as financial, technical, or commercial: <anonymized support ticket>",
     "deve_conter": ["technical"]},
]

def perguntar(prompt):
    corpo = json.dumps({"model": MODELO, "stream": False,
                        "messages": [{"role": "user", "content": prompt}]}).encode()
    req = urllib.request.Request("http://localhost:11434/api/chat", corpo,
                                 {"Content-Type": "application/json"})
    with urllib.request.urlopen(req, timeout=300) as r:
        return json.load(r)["message"]["content"]

hoje = datetime.date.today().isoformat()
acertos = 0
with open("bench_historico.jsonl", "a", encoding="utf-8") as hist:
    for t in TAREFAS:
        resposta = perguntar(t["prompt"])
        ok = all(termo.lower() in resposta.lower() for termo in t["deve_conter"])
        acertos += ok
        hist.write(json.dumps({"data": hoje, "modelo": MODELO, "tarefa": t["id"],
                               "ok": ok, "resposta": resposta}, ensure_ascii=False) + "\n")
        print(f"{t['id']:24} {'OK' if ok else 'FALHOU'}")
print(f"{hoje} {MODELO}: {acertos}/{len(TAREFAS)} — histórico em bench_historico.jsonl")

How to check: with Ollama running (ollama pull llama3.2), replace the prompts with two of your own tasks, and run it twice. The file bench_historico.jsonl should gain one line per task with each run. Read the answers marked OK: if any are poor but passed, the "deve_conter" criterion is too weak—strengthen it before trusting the score. Over time, grow from 2 to 20 or 30 tasks that represent your work.

💡 Tip

Model names, prices, and launches change every few months; anything this course mentioned by name may be different when you read it again. The method remains the same: ask which task, measure with your own cases, verify before you trust, and record the decision.

Why learn

With a radar, your own test, and a team that keeps learning, you don’t need to get predictions right: you notice change early, measure its effect on your own work, and make decisions based on data. It’s the best preparation available for a future no one knows in detail.
